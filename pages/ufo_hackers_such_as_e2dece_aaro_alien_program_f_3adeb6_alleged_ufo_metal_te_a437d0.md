## Introduction

One of the most tangible claims associated with alleged secret alien technology is the existence of physical "UFO metal" that could be tested in a laboratory. Unlike eyewitness testimony or leaked documents, a metallic specimen can be examined using established materials science techniques, making it a rare opportunity to evaluate extraordinary claims through repeatable measurements.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_aaro_alien_program_f_3adeb6_alleged_ufo_metal_te_a437d0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="UFO Metal Test illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_aaro_alien_program_f_3adeb6_alleged_ufo_metal_te_a437d0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_aaro_alien_program_f_3adeb6_alleged_ufo_metal_te_a437d0-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The best-known recent example involved a layered magnesium-based specimen promoted by researchers associated with To The Stars Academy (TTSA), who suggested it might possess exotic electromagnetic properties and could even have originated from the 1947 Roswell incident. After independent examination by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) for the US government's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the evidence pointed in a very different direction: the specimen was found to be an ordinary terrestrial magnesium alloy with conventional isotopic signatures and no measurable properties consistent with the extraordinary claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AAROs_Supplement_to_ORNLs_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf?ver=sz9XCNLG8heNHWXWQJiLgw%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO&#x27;s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic SpecimenJuly 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why the metal was promoted as extraordinary

The specimen attracted attention because of its unusual layered structure. Public advocates argued that alternating layers of magnesium, zinc, bismuth and other elements represented a deliberately engineered material unlike conventional industrial products. Some proponents further suggested that the bismuth layers could function as a terahertz (THz) waveguide—a structure capable of directing extremely high-frequency electromagnetic waves—and speculated that such behaviour might be linked to advanced propulsion concepts.

These ideas gained additional visibility after TTSA acquired the material and entered into a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) with the US Army to investigate whether any scientifically unusual properties could be demonstrated. The specimen's [chain of custody]({{ 'chain-of-custody/' | relative_url }}), however, remained incomplete, and its purported connection to Roswell was never independently verified. ORNL therefore focused on measurable physical characteristics rather than provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/ORNL-Synopsis_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf?ver=V1rMXfRV-VUb2gjTd4DzVg%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen...</span></span></span>

## What the laboratory tested

ORNL applied multiple complementary analytical techniques rather than relying on a single measurement. The investigation addressed two principal questions:

* Was the specimen of terrestrial origin?
* Did its structure support claims that it could operate as a terahertz waveguide?

### Isotopic analysis

One of the strongest tests involved measuring the isotopic ratios of magnesium and lead.

Isotopes are naturally occurring versions of the same chemical element that differ slightly in mass. Materials formed under different physical conditions can exhibit distinctive isotopic signatures. If the specimen had originated outside Earth's normal geological and industrial processes, scientists would expect unusual isotope ratios.

Instead, ORNL found that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the magnesium isotope ratios fell within the expected terrestrial range;
* the lead isotopes were likewise consistent with ordinary Earth materials;
* the overall isotopic composition was unremarkable and typical of manufactured alloys.

</div>

These findings strongly supported a terrestrial origin rather than an extraterrestrial one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AAROs_Supplement_to_ORNLs_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf?ver=sz9XCNLG8heNHWXWQJiLgw%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO&#x27;s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic SpecimenJuly 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2024</span></span></span>

### Structural and chemical analysis

Researchers also examined the specimen's internal structure using high-resolution microscopy and elemental mapping.

The analysis identified:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* alternating bands of bismuth and lead;
* magnesium grains with a conventional columnar structure;
* zinc concentrations consistent with manufactured magnesium alloys.

</div>

Although the layered appearance was visually distinctive, ORNL concluded that it reflected known metallurgical processes rather than unknown engineering methods. Similar structures can arise through established casting, rolling and experimental manufacturing techniques.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AAROs_Supplement_to_ORNLs_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf?ver=sz9XCNLG8heNHWXWQJiLgw%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO&#x27;s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic SpecimenJuly 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2024</span></span></span>

### Testing the waveguide claim

Perhaps the most widely publicised claim was that the specimen could act as a terahertz waveguide capable of supporting exotic physical effects.

Waveguides require carefully controlled geometry and electromagnetic properties. ORNL found that the specimen lacked the necessary anisotropic crystal structure and did not contain the type of continuous, single-crystal bismuth layer that theoretical proposals would require.

## What the results suggest about the specimen's origin

AARO supplemented ORNL's laboratory work with historical materials research.

Rather than identifying an alien source, the office concluded that the specimen's characteristics fit well within extensive twentieth-century research into magnesium alloys. During the Second World War and the decades that followed, engineers experimented with lightweight magnesium materials for aircraft, engines and other military applications. Many experimental manufacturing methods from that era produced layered or unconventional-looking structures without implying unknown technology.

AARO therefore assessed the specimen as consistent with historical magnesium alloy research and development, rather than evidence of recovered extraterrestrial hardware.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AAROs_Supplement_to_ORNLs_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf?ver=sz9XCNLG8heNHWXWQJiLgw%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO&#x27;s Supplement to Oak Ridge National Laboratory&#x27;s Analysis of a Metallic SpecimenJuly 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 10, 2024</span></span></span>

## How the findings affected the broader claim

The laboratory results did not identify the specimen's exact manufacturing history or original purpose. Its chain of custody remains incomplete, meaning researchers cannot state precisely where it came from or what it was originally designed to do.
