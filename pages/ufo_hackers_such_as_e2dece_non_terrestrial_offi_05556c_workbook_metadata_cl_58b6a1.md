## Introduction

The alleged Excel workbook headed **“Non-Terrestrial Officers”** has never been released, independently examined or described in a forensic report. As a result, the most valuable missing evidence is not the spreadsheet's claimed contents but its missing digital context. A filename, author record, creation date, storage location or classification marking could substantially alter how investigators interpret the document. Without those details, it is impossible to determine whether the workbook was an official personnel database, a planning exercise, a contractor working file, a training scenario, or something else entirely. McKinnon's account therefore remains a recollection of what he believed he saw rather than a document whose provenance can be verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/04/terrorist-or-ufo-truth-seeker/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">terrorist or ufo truth seeker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">? &#124; WIREDApril 28, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2006</span></span></span>

* **Original filename.** A title displayed inside an Excel workbook is not necessarily the filename stored on disk. A filename such as `NTO.xls`, `Personnel_Transfers.xls` or `Exercise_NTO.xls` would each suggest different purposes.
* **Directory path.** Knowing whether the workbook sat in a personnel directory, logistics folder, engineering project, temporary workspace or user desktop would provide immediate clues about its intended use.
* **File timestamps.** Creation, modification and access dates could show whether the workbook related to an active project, an archived programme or a short-lived exercise.
* **Document author and company fields.** Older versions of Microsoft Excel frequently stored the username, organisation and editing history within document properties unless deliberately removed.
* **Workbook properties and revision history.** Subject lines, comments, version numbers and revision counts can indicate whether a file was formally maintained or merely an informal working document.
* **Classification markings.** Headers, footers, watermarks or filename prefixes indicating classifications or handling instructions would greatly affect interpretation, although none have been independently reported.
* **Associated files.** Files located alongside the workbook—such as explanatory documents, databases or presentations—could clarify terminology that appears mysterious when viewed in isolation.

## How file context changes interpretation

The absence of metadata leaves several ordinary explanations open alongside extraordinary ones.

A workbook stored in a directory devoted to military simulations would naturally be interpreted differently from one residing in a personnel administration database. Likewise, an author field identifying a contractor developing software for a space-related project would carry different implications from one identifying an operational command.

Even the phrase "Non-Terrestrial Officers" cannot be interpreted confidently without surrounding context. It could theoretically describe personnel assigned to orbital operations, but it might equally be an internal project label, a fictional exercise, a test dataset or terminology specific to one office. McKinnon himself acknowledged in a well-known interview that the material could have related to a military game or hypothetical scenario rather than an actual secret organisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://boingboing.net/2006/06/21/interview-with-ufo-h.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: boingboing.net">[Boing Boing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">boingboing.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boing Boing Interview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary Mc Kinnon: why&#x27;d he do it?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boing BoingInterview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary McKinnon: why&#x27;d he do it? - Boing BoingJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

* whether ship names matched known databases after correcting abbreviations or [code names]({{ 'code-names/' | relative_url }});

## Why ordinary spreadsheet metadata matters so much

Modern digital investigations routinely rely on metadata because visible text rarely tells the whole story. Office documents often preserve information including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* software version;
* editing chronology;
* printer settings;
* template identifiers;
* embedded hyperlinks;
* linked data sources;
* usernames and workstation information.

</div>

Such artefacts frequently establish provenance more reliably than the document's displayed text.

Had an authenticated copy of the workbook existed, forensic examination could potentially determine whether it originated within a government office, had been copied between systems, had undergone repeated revisions or had been created as a temporary draft. None of those questions can currently be answered because no verified file has been produced for independent analysis.

## Why no public forensic inventory settles the issue

One of the strongest limitations on assessing McKinnon's claim is that there is no publicly available forensic inventory describing the alleged workbook. No court exhibit, government disclosure, authenticated screenshot or expert report has documented:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* the workbook's hash value;
* its exact storage location;
* its file size;
* its workbook properties;
* its digital signature;
* or any preserved metadata.

</div>

The legal proceedings surrounding McKinnon's hacking concentrated on the unauthorised intrusions and their consequences rather than cataloguing every document he reported seeing. ## What evidence would meaningfully change the assessment

Because the workbook itself remains unavailable, relatively modest pieces of corroborating evidence could substantially improve confidence in its provenance.

The most valuable discoveries would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an authenticated screenshot showing workbook properties;
* contemporaneous system logs identifying the accessed file;
* backup media containing the workbook;
* metadata recovered from storage devices;
* testimony from an administrator familiar with the file;
* or archival records placing a workbook with the same filename in a documented military directory.

</div>

Absent such evidence, the missing metadata remains the central obstacle to evaluating the alleged spreadsheet. Rather than merely omitting technical detail, it prevents investigators from answering the most basic forensic questions: who created the workbook, when it was created, where it belonged, what purpose it served and whether its title reflected an operational reality or something far more mundane.
