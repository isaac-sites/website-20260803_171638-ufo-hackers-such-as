## Introduction

[Mathew Bevan]({{ 'mathew-bevan/' | relative_url }}), a Welsh hacker better known online as **“Kuji”**, became associated with one of the earliest high-profile cases linking computer intrusion with a search for alleged UFO secrets. Unlike many hackers of the mid-1990s who sought prestige, financial gain or technical challenge, Bevan consistently maintained that his interest centred on claims that the United States government concealed evidence of extraterrestrial technology and advanced propulsion research. This belief shaped both his choice of targets and the routes he took through American defence and aerospace networks. Although his activities became entangled with the much broader [Rome Laboratory]({{ 'rome-laboratory/' | relative_url }}) investigation, the available evidence suggests that his target selection reflected a specific conspiracy theory rather than indiscriminate exploration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

## What Bevan believed US systems might contain

Bevan has repeatedly described becoming interested in UFO-related conspiracy theories after encountering collections of electronic documents on underground bulletin board systems during the early 1990s. In his own retrospective account, he said he discovered files alleging that groups of hackers had disappeared after probing military computers for evidence of UFO programmes. Those documents listed American military installations supposedly connected with [classified projects]({{ 'misidentified-projects/' | relative_url }}), prompting him to identify their online counterparts and attempt to access them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

According to Bevan's account, his objective was not random exploration but a systematic search for records that might support claims of a government cover-up. He later stated that he believed certain military and aerospace facilities would contain information relating to advanced propulsion, recovered technology or classified aerospace research linked by conspiracy literature to unidentified flying objects. These statements are important because they come directly from Bevan rather than from investigators or journalists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

His stated beliefs reflected ideas circulating widely in UFO communities during the early internet era. Publications such as *Phrack* and collections of conspiracy documents discussed alleged [secret projects]({{ 'secret-projects/' | relative_url }}) and military bases, although they did not provide verified evidence that such programmes actually existed. Bevan's motivation therefore rested on material he regarded as credible but which has never been substantiated by official documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

## Which military and space networks drew his attention

The organisations associated with Bevan's alleged intrusions closely matched the institutions he believed would hold classified aerospace information.

His reported targets included:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **US Air Force systems**, particularly research environments connected with the Rome Laboratory investigation, because Air Force organisations historically managed many classified aerospace projects.
* **NASA computers**, reflecting a belief that the American civilian space programme might possess imagery or technical information unavailable to the public.
* **NATO-related systems**, which conspiracy theories often portrayed as sharing highly classified defence information among allied governments.
* **Defence contractors and connected military networks**, which investigators alleged were reached through compromised accounts and interconnected systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Bevan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mathew Bevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mathew Bevan</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than treating every accessible computer as equally interesting, Bevan described identifying military installations named in UFO literature and then looking for networked systems associated with them. This pattern distinguishes his activity from opportunistic hacking aimed simply at gaining access wherever security proved weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

## Why American systems rather than British ones?

Bevan's choice of [American targets]({{ 'american-targets/' | relative_url }}) reflected a common assumption within UFO conspiracy culture: that the United States, through its military and space agencies, would be the most likely repository for any recovered extraterrestrial technology or classified UFO investigations.

This belief also helps explain why a civilian in Wales concentrated on overseas networks despite the legal and technical difficulties involved. During the early 1990s, insecure dial-up systems, weak passwords and interconnected research networks meant that geographical distance posed less of a barrier than it would today. From Bevan's perspective, American systems were simply where he expected the information to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

## What evidence exists for his stated motivation?

The evidence for Bevan's motivation comes from several different sources, but they vary considerably in strength.

The strongest evidence consists of Bevan's own later explanations. In interviews and autobiographical writing, he consistently said that UFO cover-up claims motivated his search and described how conspiracy documents guided his selection of targets. While these accounts are retrospective and therefore cannot be treated as independent proof, they have remained broadly consistent over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/confessions-of-a-hacker-by-mathew-bevan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd. » Blog Archive » Confessions of a hacker by Mathew BevanJune 26, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2008</span></span></span>

## Did Bevan find evidence of a UFO cover-up?

No publicly verified evidence indicates that Bevan uncovered proof of extraterrestrial programmes or a government UFO conspiracy.

Bevan has suggested that he encountered unusual information and later commented that certain experiences left him wondering whether some material might have represented genuine secrets rather than deliberate misinformation. However, he has never produced publicly verifiable documents demonstrating the existence of hidden UFO programmes, and no official investigation concluded that such evidence had been found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kujimedia.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kujimedia.com">[Kujimedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kujimedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kuji Media Corporation Ltd...</span></span></span>

As a result, historians of cybersecurity generally regard Bevan's case as significant because it illustrates how personal beliefs influenced target selection, not because it established the truth of those beliefs. His case remains notable within the broader history of British hackers probing American defence systems precisely because it combined a genuine security incident with an unusual and highly specific investigative motive.
