## Introduction

In discussions of UFO-related claims, including those associated with Gary McKinnon's reported discoveries on United States government systems, one of the most common reasoning errors is to assume that a file's location on an official server proves that its contents represent an official government position. It does not. [Government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}) are working environments that preserve correspondence, drafts, incoming submissions, reference material, contractor documents, rejected proposals and historical records alongside final policy documents. A genuine government file can therefore be authentic in the sense that it genuinely existed on a government system while still lacking institutional authority or evidential weight. Understanding this distinction is essential when evaluating extraordinary claims drawn from government computer systems.

## What kinds of files share official networks?

Government information systems exist to support administration, collaboration and recordkeeping rather than to publish only approved conclusions. As a result, many different categories of material coexist within the same infrastructure.

These commonly include:

* Staff recommendations that senior officials later reject.

* Training material and hypothetical scenarios.

The US National Archives distinguishes between [official records]({{ 'official-record/' | relative_url }}), non-record materials and personal papers, noting that a document's presence within an agency does not by itself determine its official status. Record status depends on how the organisation controls, uses and preserves the material, not simply where it is stored.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/scheduling/id" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesIdentification of Records, Nonrecord Materials, and Personal Papers &#124; National ArchivesJune 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 6, 2019</span></span></span>

Likewise, long-established government records-management policies define records broadly enough to include material created or received during official business while also recognising the existence of non-record copies retained merely for reference or convenience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp70-00211r000300330039-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AGENCY REGULATIONS RECORDS MANAGEMENT AND DISTRIBUTION BRANCH &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 19, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2016</span></span></span>

This means that finding an unusual document on an official network demonstrates only that the organisation possessed or handled that document at some point. It does not establish that the organisation endorsed its contents.

## Why authenticity differs from institutional authority

Authenticity and authority answer different questions.

A document may be authentic because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it genuinely existed on a government computer;
* it was genuinely created or received by government personnel; or
* it genuinely formed part of an agency's recordkeeping process.

</div>

Authority asks something different:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the document approved?
* Did it become official policy?
* Was it supported by evidence?
* Did the responsible organisation adopt its conclusions?

</div>

These questions require contextual information that cannot be answered merely from the server on which a file resided.

Government agencies themselves recognise this distinction. For example, the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency notes that only full and final material is eligible for public-release review, and explicitly warns that official information appearing publicly should not automatically be regarded as approved for public release or as unclassified simply because it exists outside restricted systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nga.mil/resources/Public_Release.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nga.mil">[National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nga.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geospatial-Intelligence AgencyPublic Release &#124; National Geospatial-Intelligence AgencyJune 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

The same principle applies internally. Drafts often circulate through official systems precisely because they are still under discussion.

## Government archives preserve the decision-making process

Modern records management is designed to preserve how decisions were made, not merely the final outcome.

This means archival collections routinely contain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* competing versions of the same document;
* staff comments;
* recommendations;
* memoranda requesting approval;
* proposed revisions;
* rejected alternatives.

</div>

Historical CIA records illustrate this process. Numerous documents preserved in the CIA Reading Room are explicitly identified as regulations, reports, draft handbooks or administrative material documenting ongoing internal processes rather than final agency positions. Their preservation reflects records-management requirements, not automatic institutional endorsement of every statement they contain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp70-00211r000300330039-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AGENCY REGULATIONS RECORDS MANAGEMENT AND DISTRIBUTION BRANCH &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)December 19, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 19, 2016</span></span></span>

For historians, this is valuable because it reveals how government organisations worked. For readers searching for extraordinary claims, however, it creates a significant risk of mistaking process documents for authoritative conclusions.

## Why this matters when interpreting UFO-related material

The distinction becomes especially important in discussions surrounding Gary McKinnon.

McKinnon has stated that during his [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to US military and NASA systems he encountered material suggesting hidden space programmes and unidentified craft. Because the alleged material was reportedly located on genuine government computers, some commentators have argued that this alone validates the underlying claims.

That conclusion does not follow.

Even assuming a file genuinely existed on an official system, several alternative explanations remain possible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it may have been an incoming attachment from another organisation;
* it may have represented one employee's working notes;
* it may have summarised a claim for evaluation rather than acceptance;
* it may have been incomplete or taken out of context;
* it may have been superseded or rejected.

</div>

Without metadata identifying the document's author, approval history, distribution, surrounding correspondence and relationship to other records, the organisational meaning of an isolated file cannot be reliably determined.

This limitation is one reason investigators and archivists place substantial emphasis on provenance—the documented history of a record—rather than on server location alone.

## Which contextual records are needed before drawing conclusions?

Determining whether a government document represents institutional authority requires more than verifying that it came from an official system.

Useful contextual evidence includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Authorship:** Who created the document?
* **Purpose:** Was it informational, advisory, administrative or operational?
* **Approval history:** Was it signed, approved or formally adopted?
* **Version history:** Is it an early draft or the final version?
* **Related correspondence:** Do emails or memoranda explain how it was received or evaluated?
* **Distribution:** Who received it, and why?
* **Subsequent actions:** Did later documents implement, revise or reject its recommendations?

</div>

Without this surrounding record, an isolated file can easily be misunderstood.

Archives preserve relationships between documents because meaning often emerges only when the complete documentary chain is examined rather than a single page viewed in isolation.

## A genuine server is not a seal of truth

Official computer systems authenticate location, not conclusions.

A document stored on a government server may indeed be genuine in the narrow sense that it really formed part of an agency's digital environment. However, that authenticity does not establish that the government accepted its claims, regarded them as accurate or incorporated them into official policy.

For controversial subjects such as alleged UFO evidence, this distinction is fundamental. The strongest historical and archival practice is to evaluate not only where a document was found, but also who wrote it, why it existed, how it moved through institutional processes and whether the organisation ultimately endorsed its contents. Only by examining that broader context can researchers distinguish authentic government records from authoritative government positions.

      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }

  }

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
