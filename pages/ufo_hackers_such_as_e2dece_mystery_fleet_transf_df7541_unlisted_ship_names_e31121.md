## Introduction

Gary McKinnon's recollection that he encountered ship names which could not be found in publicly available US Navy listings has often been presented as one of the strongest parts of his wider claim about a hidden space fleet. On closer examination, however, the absence of a vessel name from a public database proves remarkably little. Naval records are incomplete, ships are identified in several different ways, names can change during a vessel's life, and many military or government-operated craft never appear in the same reference sources. Without the original spreadsheet or the actual names, it is impossible to determine whether McKinnon had encountered classified vessels, ordinary administrative shorthand, obsolete records or something else entirely.

## Why an Unlisted Ship Is Not Proof

The key logical step in McKinnon's reasoning was straightforward: if a ship name could not be located in public Navy sources, it was reasonable to suspect that the vessel belonged to a secret programme. That inference is understandable but considerably stronger than the evidence supports.

An unsuccessful search does not distinguish between several possibilities. It may indicate that the search terms were incomplete, that the record used a different naming convention, that the vessel was not a commissioned US Navy warship, or simply that the relevant public catalogue did not include it. Only if these more ordinary explanations can be excluded would secrecy become the leading explanation.

Because McKinnon no longer remembers the names he searched for, none of those possibilities can now be tested.

## Limits of Public Naval Databases in the Early 2000s

The principal public catalogue for US Navy ships is the Naval Vessel Register (NVR), maintained by the Naval Sea Systems Command. Although authoritative, it has never claimed to be a complete historical database of every vessel ever associated with the Navy. The register explicitly notes that ships and service craft disposed of before 1987 were not fully included and have only been added gradually over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Resources/Naval-Vessel-Register/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navsea.navy.mil">[NAVSEA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navsea.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register</span></span></span>

That limitation matters because someone searching the register in the early 2000s would have encountered an even less complete historical resource than exists today. A missing result could simply reflect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* incomplete historical digitisation;
* gradual updates to older records;
* administrative omissions;
* vessels outside the register's intended scope.

</div>

Consequently, "not found" was never equivalent to "does not exist".

## Prefixes, Hull Numbers and Alternate Vessel Identities

Ship names are only one way the US Navy identifies vessels. Administrative documents frequently rely on hull classification symbols and hull numbers—for example, DDG-51 for a guided-missile destroyer or SSN-774 for a nuclear-powered attack submarine. Internal logistics systems may emphasise these identifiers because they are more precise than names, particularly when names are reused across generations of ships. The Naval Vessel Register itself organises information by hull classification as well as by name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Resources/Naval-Vessel-Register/NVR-Ships/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navsea.navy.mil">[NAVSEA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navsea.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register &gt; NVR Ships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register &gt; NVR Ships</span></span></span>

Several additional complications make name searches unreliable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Renaming:** Government vessels can receive new names during their service lives.
* **Transfer between organisations:** Ships may move between the Navy, Military Sealift Command, the Maritime Administration or allied governments.
* **Different prefixes:** USS, USNS and other designations identify different categories of vessel, while some auxiliary craft are primarily known by their hull numbers.
* **Administrative abbreviations:** Internal spreadsheets often use shortened names, project identifiers or locally understood abbreviations rather than formal titles.

</div>

Without seeing the original entries, there is no way to know which naming convention the spreadsheet employed.

## Ordinary Reasons a Ship Name May Not Appear

Even if McKinnon's searches were conducted carefully, several mundane explanations remain plausible.

A vessel might have been:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an auxiliary rather than a commissioned warship;
* temporarily assigned under another organisation;
* listed primarily under a hull number rather than a name;
* recorded under an abbreviated or unofficial designation;
* renamed before or after the period covered by the spreadsheet;
* omitted from the online reference material available at the time.

</div>

[Military exercises]({{ 'exercise-files/' | relative_url }}) also generate planning documents that include placeholder names, notional units and fictional force structures. McKinnon himself acknowledged in later interviews that what he saw could conceivably have been part of a military game or exercise rather than an operational secret. That concession significantly weakens the argument that unfamiliar names necessarily indicated an undisclosed fleet.

## What the Missing Names Actually Tell Us

The inability to locate a ship name is therefore weak evidence on its own. In historical research, the value of an unidentified name depends heavily on whether the original document survives, whether multiple independent records corroborate it, and whether researchers can establish precisely how the identifier was used.

In McKinnon's case, none of those conditions can be met. The spreadsheet has never been produced, no screenshots preserve the vessel names, and McKinnon has repeatedly stated that he cannot now remember them. As a result, the claim cannot progress beyond recollection.

The missing names therefore support only a limited conclusion: McKinnon believed he encountered vessel identifiers that he could not reconcile with public Navy sources. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate the existence of a classified naval fleet, because incomplete public records, alternate naming conventions, administrative shorthand and fictional planning data all provide ordinary explanations that remain impossible to rule out.
