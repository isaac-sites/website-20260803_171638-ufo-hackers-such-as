## Introduction

Stopping extradition does not necessarily mean abandoning justice. In cases such as Gary McKinnon's, the central question is whether accountability can be achieved through prosecution in the defendant's home country rather than by surrendering them to a foreign state. A domestic trial may preserve the [public interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}) in enforcing the law while reducing avoidable harms associated with overseas detention, prolonged separation from family, disruption of established medical care and exposure to an unfamiliar legal and prison system. The issue is therefore not whether alleged cybercrime should go unpunished, but whether the location of prosecution is proportionate when more than one country has a legitimate claim to [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}). UK law increasingly recognises that, where this is realistically possible, domestic prosecution can be a fair and effective alternative rather than a concession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/jurisdiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Jurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceJurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceJune 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2025</span></span></span>

## Can a Domestic Trial Deliver Justice Without Extradition?

The McKinnon case is often misunderstood as a choice between extradition and impunity. Legally, those are not the only options. If conduct committed from the United Kingdom also amounts to offences under UK law, domestic authorities may consider prosecution in British courts, provided jurisdiction exists and the available evidence satisfies the normal prosecutorial tests. The Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) expressly recognises that cases involving concurrent UK and overseas jurisdiction require an assessment of which forum best serves the interests of justice rather than an automatic preference for extradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/jurisdiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Jurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceJurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceJune 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2025</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because the objectives of criminal justice—establishing responsibility, protecting the public and imposing proportionate sanctions—can often be achieved without requiring an accused person to stand trial abroad. In cases where the alleged conduct occurred largely from a computer in the UK, domestic proceedings may provide a practical route to accountability while avoiding some of the additional human consequences associated with international surrender.

## Accountability Without Overseas Surrender

A domestic prosecution does not reduce the seriousness of alleged offences. Rather, it changes the venue in which responsibility is determined.

For defendants with significant medical or psychiatric vulnerabilities, trying the case at home may preserve continuity of treatment, access to established healthcare providers and family support. These considerations became particularly important in McKinnon's case because the Home Secretary concluded that extradition itself created an exceptionally high risk to his life. That decision did not question the seriousness of the allegations but assessed whether the particular method of prosecution would be compatible with his [human rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement - GOV.UK...</span></span></span>

Domestic proceedings may also reduce practical disadvantages that have little connection with guilt or innocence, including:

* prolonged separation from close family and support networks;
* disruption of specialist [mental health]({{ 'mental-health/' | relative_url }}) treatment;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* unfamiliar prison regimes and legal procedures;
* increased costs and obstacles for defence preparation; and
* lengthy periods of overseas pre-trial detention where applicable.

</div>

None of these factors automatically outweigh the requesting state's interest in prosecution. They are instead relevant to assessing whether equivalent criminal justice objectives can be achieved through less intrusive means.

## Practical Barriers to Transferring Evidence and Charges

A common objection is that cybercrime frequently affects victims located overseas, making foreign prosecution seem inevitable. In reality, international criminal investigations routinely involve evidence-sharing between jurisdictions.

The Council of Europe's Convention on Cybercrime establishes mechanisms for mutual legal assistance, preservation of electronic evidence and cooperation between national authorities. These frameworks exist precisely because digital offences often cross borders, allowing evidence to be shared even when prosecution occurs in a different jurisdiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.coe.int/en/web/cybercrime/competent-authorities" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coe.int">[Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coe.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Portal Competent authorities under the Convention</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PortalCompetent authorities under the Convention - Cybercrime...</span></span></span>

That does not mean every case is suitable for domestic prosecution. Prosecutors must consider practical questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether the necessary digital evidence can legally be obtained and presented in a UK court;
* whether witnesses located overseas can provide evidence effectively;
* where the principal victims and financial losses are located;
* whether multiple suspects are being prosecuted together elsewhere; and
* whether parallel proceedings would create unnecessary duplication.

</div>

The CPS guidance reflects these considerations when discussing concurrent jurisdiction and forum decisions. Evidence availability, delay, victim interests and the practicality of conducting a single coherent prosecution are all relevant factors rather than automatic reasons favouring one country over another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/jurisdiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Jurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceJurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceJune 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2025</span></span></span>

## The Forum Question Is About Justice, Not Nationality

The debate is sometimes portrayed as whether British citizens should simply be tried in Britain. That is not the legal test.

The UK's "forum bar", introduced after prolonged controversy over cases including McKinnon's, requires courts to consider whether a substantial part of the alleged criminal activity occurred in the United Kingdom and whether, taking specified interests of justice into account, prosecution should occur domestically instead of abroad. Those factors include the location of harm, victim interests, availability of evidence, possible delay, the practicality of conducting one prosecution, and the defendant's connections with the UK.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/jurisdiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Jurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceJurisdiction &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceJune 2, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2025</span></span></span>

This framework avoids treating citizenship as decisive. A foreign national with strong UK connections may benefit from similar considerations, while a British citizen whose alleged conduct is overwhelmingly centred abroad may still face extradition if that better serves justice.

## Alternatives to Extradition That Preserve Public Protection

Rejecting extradition need not leave courts without meaningful responses if wrongdoing is proved.

Depending on the offence and sentence imposed under domestic law, alternatives may include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* prosecution before UK criminal courts;
* imprisonment where justified by the seriousness of the conduct;
* community-based sentences with strict supervision;
* treatment requirements where clinically appropriate;
* restrictions on computer use or internet access;
* financial penalties or confiscation orders where available; and
* probation supervision designed to reduce the risk of further offending.

</div>

The appropriate outcome depends on the offence, the offender's circumstances and sentencing law rather than on the fact that extradition was refused. Mental illness or neurodevelopmental conditions may affect sentencing and management, but they do not eliminate criminal responsibility by themselves.

## Why the McKinnon Debate Continues to Matter

The McKinnon case continues to influence wider debates because it highlighted that international cybercrime prosecutions involve more than technical questions of jurisdiction. They also require balancing effective law enforcement against proportionality and individual rights.

A domestic trial will not always be the correct solution. Some cases genuinely require prosecution in the country where the principal harm occurred or where related defendants are being tried. Nevertheless, where substantial conduct took place in the United Kingdom, the evidence can realistically be presented before a British court, and domestic prosecution can protect the public while avoiding disproportionate human consequences, trying the case at home may better reconcile accountability with fairness.
