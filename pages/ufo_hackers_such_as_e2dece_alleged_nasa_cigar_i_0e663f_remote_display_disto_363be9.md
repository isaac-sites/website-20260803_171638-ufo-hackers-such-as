## Introduction

Gary McKinnon's description of a smooth, cigar-shaped object is inseparable from the way he said he viewed it. By his own account, he did not examine the original high-resolution [NASA image]({{ 'nasa-image/' | relative_url }}) directly. Instead, he watched only part of it appear over a slow 56-kilobit-per-second dial-up connection while using a remote desktop configured to four-bit colour and a very low screen resolution. Those display limitations are not a minor detail: they are a plausible mechanism by which an ordinary object could appear unusually simple, seamless and elongated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://seclists.org/isn/2006/Jun/105" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: seclists.org">[SecLists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">seclists.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sec Lists Information Security News: &#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Information Security News: &#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

This does not prove that the object was conventional, nor does it prove McKinnon's interpretation was wrong. It does, however, mean that any assessment of the alleged image has to account for how aggressive image simplification can change the appearance of shape, texture and edges before the viewer ever interprets what they are seeing.

## What Four-Bit Colour and Low Resolution Remove

A four-bit display is limited to just 16 colours on screen. Combined with reduced screen resolution, this dramatically lowers the amount of visual information that can be presented at any one time. Fine tonal transitions become bands, subtle shadows disappear and small surface details merge into larger blocks of colour.

* Small panel lines could disappear.
* Rivets or fasteners could merge into surrounding surfaces.

## How Banding and Edge Simplification Alter Silhouettes

Human vision identifies objects largely from their outlines. When image quality deteriorates, internal detail is lost before the overall silhouette disappears.

This creates several effects that can exaggerate the impression of a perfectly streamlined object.

First, edge smoothing causes neighbouring pixels to merge. Instead of seeing multiple structural components, the viewer may perceive one continuous body.

Second, colour banding reduces gradual lighting differences. Cylindrical spacecraft modules, solar-array supports or payload structures that would normally be distinguished by changing highlights can visually merge into a single elongated form.

Third, incomplete image rendering may leave only the most prominent portions visible. McKinnon stated that he saw only part of a very large image before the session ended. If only the central section had fully rendered, attached structures might have been absent or only partially displayed, further reinforcing the impression of a simple cigar-like shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://seclists.org/isn/2006/Jun/105" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: seclists.org">[SecLists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">seclists.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sec Lists Information Security News: &#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Information Security News: &#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He FoundJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

These effects are well understood in digital imaging and remote display systems. Reduced graphical fidelity routinely sacrifices detail in favour of transmitting only coarse visual information, especially over low-bandwidth connections.

## Which Visual Details Could Not Survive the Connection

One of McKinnon's most frequently quoted observations was that the object showed "no visible seams or riveting." That statement is often treated as evidence that the object differed from conventional spacecraft.

From an imaging perspective, however, the absence of visible seams is exactly what would be expected if the original photograph contained much than the remote display could reproduce.

Features especially vulnerable to disappearing include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* narrow seams between structural panels;
* rows of rivets or bolts;
* thermal blanket wrinkles;
* small inspection hatches;
* cable runs and attachment points;
* surface weathering or subtle texture;
* minor shadows cast by projecting equipment.

</div>

The inability to see these features therefore cannot distinguish between an advanced seamless craft and a conventional spacecraft viewed under severe display constraints. It only establishes that the transmitted image lacked sufficient detail to resolve them.

## Why Partial Rendering Matters

Remote desktop software of the early 2000s was designed to minimise bandwidth by updating only the portions of the screen that had changed. Large images could appear progressively, often refreshing block by block rather than arriving as a complete frame.

McKinnon described watching a huge image slowly emerge before the connection ended. Under those circumstances, several distortions become possible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the image may have been incomplete;
* some regions may have remained at lower quality than others;
* sections could have refreshed at different times;
* the visible portion may not have represented the final rendered image.

</div>

This is important because human perception naturally fills missing information. Once the brain recognises an elongated outline, it tends to interpret incomplete visual data as belonging to a single coherent object rather than a partially rendered collection of structures.

## Why the Shape Alone Cannot Identify the Object

"Cigar-shaped" is a descriptive impression rather than a technical classification. Many ordinary aerospace objects—including satellites, payload modules, rocket stages and spacecraft components—can appear elongated depending on viewing angle, lighting, cropping and image quality.

When severe display limitations remove fine structural information, different objects become more visually similar. Distinctive engineering features that normally allow identification disappear first, leaving only the broad silhouette.

Consequently, the reported shape provides relatively weak evidence about the object's true identity. It is consistent with McKinnon faithfully describing what he saw on his screen, while remaining insufficient to determine whether the original high-resolution image depicted an unconventional craft, a conventional spacecraft or another object entirely.
