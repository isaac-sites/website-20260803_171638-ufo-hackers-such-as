## Introduction

## The document itself has never surfaced

The decisive weakness is the absence of the file. McKinnon did not release the spreadsheet, reproduce its contents or provide a complete list of its entries. No journalist, investigator or independent technical examiner is known to have authenticated a copy.

McKinnon’s computers were seized after his arrest, and he said one hard drive was sent to the United States. Yet no publicly available prosecution record identifies a recovered “Non-Terrestrial Officers” spreadsheet or confirms that such a file was found among the data he accessed or copied. The US indictment documents [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA computers, but it concentrates on intrusion methods, affected systems and alleged damage rather than confirming the UFO-related material McKinnon later described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud...Gary McKinnon accessed and damaged without authorization 92...</span></span></span>

Criminal indictments are not inventories of every file viewed by an intruder, and officials would not necessarily discuss sensitive material publicly. The missing file also prevents elementary verification. Researchers cannot examine its metadata, worksheet tabs, abbreviations, organisational codes, creation date or surrounding directory. They cannot check whether the names belonged to real officers, fictional exercise participants, contractors, astronauts, satellite controllers or people assigned to classified but terrestrial programmes. They cannot even establish whether “Non-Terrestrial Officers” was the formal title of a record, an informal heading added by one employee or wording McKinnon reconstructed from memory.

## “Non-terrestrial” does not necessarily mean alien

The phrase is striking because ordinary readers often treat “non-terrestrial” as synonymous with “extraterrestrial”. In government, aerospace and communications language, however, it can simply mean something situated above or operating away from the Earth’s surface.

Modern technical literature uses “non-terrestrial networks” for communications involving satellites, high-altitude platforms and airborne systems. Such terminology distinguishes spaceborne or aerial infrastructure from ground-based networks without implying alien origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04590" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv A brief</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A brief introduction to satellite communications for Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN)May 8, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2023</span></span></span>

A military spreadsheet could therefore have used “non-terrestrial” to distinguish personnel associated with orbital operations, satellite systems, space surveillance, exercises or simulated commands from personnel assigned to conventional ground units. That would still be interesting, particularly in the early 2000s, when US military planning increasingly emphasised space as an operational domain. It would not by itself demonstrate a secret extraterrestrial presence.

Other ordinary possibilities include:

* **Personnel not assigned to a terrestrial command.** The heading may have referred to officers attached to space-based missions, satellite operations or a planning cell.
* **Exercise or simulation data.** [Military exercises]({{ 'exercise-files/' | relative_url }}) frequently use invented units, vessels and personnel to avoid confusing scenarios with real operational records.

References to ship-to-ship transfers are often presented as the document’s second major clue. The wording does not establish that. “Ship” can refer to naval vessels, spacecraft, support craft or fictional platforms in an exercise. Unfamiliar names could belong to classified vessels, foreign units, proposed platforms, computer-generated entities or aliases used in training. Without the actual entries, the claim cannot be tested against naval registers, spaceflight records or exercise documentation.

The relationship between the two parts of the spreadsheet is also unclear. McKinnon’s interviews do not establish whether the officer list and transfer information appeared on the same worksheet, on separate tabs within one workbook or merely among several spreadsheets accessed during the same session. This detail matters because later summaries often depict a single integrated manifest containing officers, spacecraft and movements. The surviving contemporary account is less precise: it reports a spreadsheet title, names and ranks, and transfer information, but not the record’s full structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He claims to have discovered a NASA department that airbrushes UFO images from high-resolution photos and an Excel spreadsheet titled &quot;No...</span></span></span>

Similarly, the phrase is sometimes transformed from “ship-to-ship transfers” into “fleet-to-fleet transfers”. The latter wording sounds more like a large organised space navy, but McKinnon’s widely cited Wired interview used “ship-to-ship”. Treating every later variant as exact risks building an elaborate narrative from language that has shifted in repetition.

## Why the officers could not be checked

McKinnon said the listed Air Force personnel were “not registered anywhere else”, but he did not explain a comprehensive verification method. In the early 2000s, there was no complete public database of all serving US military personnel and assignments. Classified posts, intelligence work, temporary duties, name variations and incomplete online records would all make an officer difficult to identify.

Even possessing the names would not automatically resolve the issue. A person’s absence from public search results would not show that the person was fictitious or stationed off-world. Conversely, identifying some names as real space-operations personnel would not necessarily validate the more extraordinary interpretation; it might instead support a conventional explanation involving satellite or orbital work.

The absence of the names is therefore especially damaging. It eliminates the most obvious route to corroboration. Researchers cannot compare ranks, service histories, assignments or organisational affiliations, nor determine whether the claimed entries followed genuine Air Force personnel formats.

This creates a fundamental evidence problem: McKinnon’s interpretation partly depended on unsuccessful searches, but neither the original search terms nor the underlying names are available for replication.

A credible breakthrough would require more than another retelling. The most valuable evidence would be the original spreadsheet or a forensic copy retaining metadata and its directory context. What it cannot presently bear is the confident claim that McKinnon uncovered a verified roster of officers serving in a secret extraterrestrial fleet.

## The lasting lesson of the document

The “Non-Terrestrial Officers” story endures because it combines a verified breach with an unverifiable discovery. The first fact lends credibility to the possibility that McKinnon saw sensitive information; the absence of the second object leaves almost unlimited room for interpretation.

Its importance within the history of UFO-motivated hacking is therefore less as proof of a concealed space force than as a demonstration of how ambiguous digital material can become folklore once removed from its technical setting. A spreadsheet title, unfamiliar names and unexplained transfers might have been highly revealing in context. Stripped of the file, metadata and surrounding records, they are clues without a stable meaning.

The most defensible reading keeps both sides in view: McKinnon may accurately have remembered an oddly titled military spreadsheet, but the public evidence does not establish that its officers were extraterrestrial, stationed beyond Earth or connected to a secret space fleet.
