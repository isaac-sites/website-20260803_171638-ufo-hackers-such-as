## Introduction

The key distinction in the cases of Gary McKinnon, Mathew Bevan and [Richard Pryce]({{ 'richard-pryce/' | relative_url }}) is that the computer intrusions were established through technical investigation, whereas the alleged UFO-related discoveries were never independently verified. Investigators, prosecutors and courts accepted that [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to government and military systems had occurred. By contrast, no authenticated alien document, confirmed extraterrestrial image or verified evidence of advanced non-human technology emerged from any of the three cases. This difference is central to understanding why the incidents remain historically important as cybersecurity events while continuing to generate debate within UFO circles.

## Technical evidence confirming unauthorised access

The evidence supporting the intrusions themselves is extensive and comes from conventional forensic and legal sources rather than from later recollections.

In McKinnon's case, United States prosecutors alleged that between 2001 and 2002 he gained unauthorised access to dozens of Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA computers, obtained administrative privileges, installed remote-access software, copied password files and disrupted military networks. The indictment described specific systems affected, investigative agencies involved and estimated financial losses, all reflecting a documented computer-crime investigation rather than speculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

Similarly, the [Rome Laboratory]({{ 'rome-laboratory/' | relative_url }}) incident involving [Mathew Bevan]({{ 'mathew-bevan/' | relative_url }}) and Richard Pryce is well documented in cybersecurity history. Investigators traced how compromised US Air Force systems were used as stepping stones into other military, academic and research networks. The incident prompted extensive forensic work and became an influential example of the security weaknesses of the early internet. The historical significance of the case lies in demonstrating how poorly protected interconnected systems could be exploited, not in confirming any extraordinary discovery.

Importantly, none of the official investigations into either the McKinnon or Rome Laboratory cases concluded that classified UFO material had been obtained. The verified evidence concerns:

* successful unauthorised access;
* compromised military and [government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }});
* forensic traces of intrusion;
* legal proceedings arising from those intrusions.

Those findings are supported by technical investigation and [court records]({{ 'court-record/' | relative_url }}) rather than witness recollections alone.

## The absence of authenticated UFO files from Rome

Although Bevan has been associated with an interest in UFO secrecy, the Rome Laboratory investigation did not produce authenticated documents demonstrating that alien technology or extraterrestrial programmes existed.

Later retellings sometimes imply that the hackers uncovered hidden UFO archives, but the available evidence does not support this claim. Contemporary reporting focused on the scale of the security breach, the risk posed by compromised military computers and investigators' efforts to understand how attackers moved through interconnected networks. No official report released authenticated UFO files, and no independently verified cache of extraordinary material has been linked to the Rome Laboratory intrusion.

This absence is significant because it distinguishes motivation from outcome. A hacker may search for particular information without ever finding it. The documented investigation establishes that networks were entered, but it does not establish that extraordinary material was discovered inside them.

## Why McKinnon's remembered findings remain disputed

Gary McKinnon's case differs because the UFO claims originate primarily from his own later descriptions of what he remembers seeing while browsing NASA and military systems.

Among his most widely repeated claims are that he briefly viewed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a spreadsheet referring to "non-terrestrial officers";
* an image of a cigar-shaped object lacking conventional aircraft features;
* evidence that NASA maintained both processed and unprocessed satellite imagery.

</div>

These accounts have been repeated in interviews over many years and are consistent in broad outline. However, they remain personal testimony rather than independently verifiable evidence. McKinnon has explained that he did not preserve screenshots or copies of the alleged material before losing access, leaving no primary evidence available for external examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Because no authenticated files have ever been produced, investigators, historians and journalists cannot test whether the remembered documents existed as described, whether they were misunderstood, or whether they had entirely conventional explanations. The claims therefore occupy a fundamentally different evidential category from the documented network intrusions themselves.

## The proof gap between access and discovery

The comparison between McKinnon, Bevan and Pryce illustrates an important evidential principle: proving access to information is not the same as proving the contents of that information.

In all three cases:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* unauthorised computer access is supported by technical evidence, investigative records and legal proceedings;
* interest in UFO secrecy is documented to varying degrees through statements or later accounts;
* authenticated alien documents or confirmed extraterrestrial technology have never been produced from the intrusions.

</div>

This distinction explains why cybersecurity historians generally regard the cases as landmark examples of insecure government networks, while UFO researchers continue to debate the meaning of claims that cannot be independently examined.

The same distinction is reflected in current government approaches to unidentified anomalous phenomena. Modern scientific and governmental investigations emphasise that extraordinary claims require verifiable data, reproducible evidence and authenticated documentation rather than anecdotal recollection alone. NASA has likewise stated that there is currently no verified evidence that reported unidentified anomalous phenomena represent alien technology, highlighting the need for higher-quality observational data before such conclusions can be drawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA ScienceMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>

## What was proven and what remains a claim?

Taken together, the three cases demonstrate a clear proof gap.

What has been established beyond reasonable dispute is that British hackers successfully entered poorly protected American government and military computer systems during the 1990s and early 2000s. That conclusion rests on forensic investigations, admissions, indictments and [official records]({{ 'official-record/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

What has **not** been established is that those intrusions uncovered authenticated evidence of extraterrestrial spacecraft, alien personnel or concealed non-human technology. Those assertions depend principally on personal testimony—especially McKinnon's recollections—without surviving documents or independently verifiable artefacts. As a result, the strongest evidence across all three cases concerns the reality of the computer intrusions themselves, while the most dramatic UFO-related claims remain unverified.
