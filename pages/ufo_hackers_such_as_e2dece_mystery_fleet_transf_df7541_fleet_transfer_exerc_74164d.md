## Introduction

One of the most conventional explanations for Gary McKinnon's reported "fleet-to-fleet transfers" is that he encountered data created for a [military exercise]({{ 'exercise-files/' | relative_url }}), war game or planning scenario rather than records of real operations. Importantly, McKinnon himself has acknowledged this possibility. While he believed the unfamiliar ship names and the phrase "Non-Terrestrial Officers" pointed towards a hidden programme, he also admitted that he could not be certain whether the spreadsheet represented an actual operational system or a hypothetical exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

This matters because modern armed forces routinely generate realistic fictional documentation. Exercises are designed to resemble genuine operational activity, complete with personnel lists, logistics movements, fleet transfers, invented unit names and fictional geopolitical scenarios. Without the original spreadsheet—or even the ship names themselves—it is impossible to determine whether McKinnon saw records of genuine movements, planning documents or training material.

## McKinnon's Own Concession That It Could Have Been a Game

An often-overlooked part of McKinnon's account appears in his 2006 interview with *Wired*. After describing the spreadsheet headed "Non-Terrestrial Officers" and its references to ship-to-ship transfers, interviewer Noah Shachtman asked whether the material might simply have been "some sort of military strategy game or outline of hypothetical situations."

Rather than rejecting the suggestion, McKinnon accepted that possibility. He replied that it "could have been" and acknowledged he could not prove otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why Military Exercises Produce Convincing Administrative Records

Large military exercises are intended to train commanders, logisticians and operational staffs under realistic conditions. That realism extends far beyond simulated battles.

Planning documents frequently include:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/policy/army/fm/25-4/Ch2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* fictional personnel rosters;
* logistics spreadsheets;
* equipment movement records;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/bibliographies-and-research-guides/research-guides/inventory-naval-records-collection-office-naval-records-library/appendix-i-inventory-naval-records-collection-office-naval-records-library.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[history.navy.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mil Appendix I</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mil Appendix I</span></span></span>
* deployment schedules;
* transfer orders;
* supply requests;
* fleet assignments;
* administrative tracking documents.

</div>

The paperwork often resembles genuine operational records because participants must practise using the same administrative systems they would employ during real deployments. Government guidance on exercise planning specifically emphasises creating realistic scenarios with clearly defined organisations, responsibilities and operational processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/exercising-best-practice-guidance/exercising-best-practice-guidance-html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exercising Best Practice Guidance (HTML</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exercising Best Practice Guidance (HTML</span></span></span>

For someone encountering isolated files without surrounding context, such documents can appear indistinguishable from records of actual military activity.

## Fictional Units and Invented Ship Names Are Normal

Another feature supporting the exercise explanation is the routine use of fictional organisations.

Military training regularly invents:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/879271334/075-003-BI-SCD-Collective-Training-and-Exercise-Exercise-Process-01Sept2023" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[scribd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

* countries;
* fleets;
* military headquarters;
* command structures;
* vessels;
* aircraft;
* intelligence organisations.

These fictional entities allow planners to rehearse operations without identifying genuine classified capabilities or inadvertently creating diplomatic complications.

A contemporary example comes from Royal Navy Operational Sea Training. For decades, British and allied warships trained within fictional geopolitical scenarios involving invented states such as "Brownia" and "Ginger". The names, military situations and operational documents existed solely for training while closely imitating real-world naval operations. In 2023 the Royal Navy replaced this long-running fictional setting with a new exercise scenario better reflecting current strategic conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.royalnavy.mod.uk/news/2023/september/06/20230906-peace-between-brownia-and-ginger-means-a-challenging-new-scenario-for-ships-undertaking-ost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalnavy.mod.uk">[Royal Navy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalnavy.mod.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal NavyPeace between Brownia and Ginger means a challenging new scenario for ships undertaking OSTSeptember 6, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2023</span></span></span>

Similarly, NATO's Joint Warfare Centre develops comprehensive fictional scenarios containing synthetic governments, military organisations and supporting documentation specifically designed to appear operationally realistic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jwc.nato.int/our-work/scenario-development/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jwc.nato.int">[NATO Joint Warfare Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jwc.nato.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Joint Warfare Centre Scenario Development: 360-Degree Multi-Domain Setting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NATO Joint Warfare CentreScenario Development: 360-Degree Multi-Domain Setting - NATO&#x27;s JWC...</span></span></span>

## Would "Fleet-to-Fleet Transfers" Fit Exercise Planning?

The phrase itself is not unusual within military logistics.

Exercises frequently model movements of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* personnel between commands;
* equipment between formations;
* ammunition and supplies;
* maintenance assets;
* replacement crews;
* repair materials.

</div>

Logistics staff are often evaluated on their ability to manage these movements under simulated operational conditions. Consequently, spreadsheets recording transfers between fleets or commands would not be surprising inside a planning or exercise environment.

McKinnon later described the records as involving "materiel" transfers—a standard military term for equipment and supplies rather than personnel alone. That wording arguably fits logistics planning at least as naturally as it fits a hidden operational fleet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectcamelot.org/lang/en/gary_mckinnon_interview_transcript_en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectcamelot.org">[Project Camelot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectcamelot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on projectcamelot.org.</span></span></span>

## Does the Exercise Theory Explain Every Detail?

Not completely.

Supporters of McKinnon's interpretation argue that several features remain unusual:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the heading "Non-Terrestrial Officers";<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://cybernews.com/news/nasa-gary-mckinnon-hacking-ufo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cybernews.com">[cybernews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cybernews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“Non-terrestrial officers:” the UFO files McKinnon found, hacking NASA​ &#124; CybernewsMarch 2, 2026 — “NON-TERRESTRIAL OFFICERS:” THE UFO FI...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>
* apparently unfamiliar ship names;
* the combination of officer lists and transfer records;
* McKinnon's inability to identify the vessels publicly.

</div>

However, each point also has conventional possibilities.

"Non-terrestrial" need not refer to extraterrestrials. It could indicate personnel assigned away from Earth's surface, personnel associated with satellite programmes, a project-specific label, or simply an unusual internal naming convention. McKinnon himself has suggested that the phrase might have referred to astronauts rather than aliens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Likewise, unfamiliar ship names do not automatically indicate secret spacecraft. Exercise planners deliberately create fictional platforms, while administrative systems may also contain temporary project names, placeholders or test records.

The available evidence does not allow these alternatives to be separated confidently.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mystery_fleet_transf_df7541_fleet_transfer_exerc_74164d-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Exercise Theory illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mystery_fleet_transf_df7541_fleet_transfer_exerc_74164d-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mystery_fleet_transf_df7541_fleet_transfer_exerc_74164d-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## What Evidence Would Distinguish Planning Data from Real Operations?

If the original spreadsheet still existed, several features could help determine whether it described an exercise or genuine operations.

Evidence favouring an exercise would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* references to scenario control organisations;
* fictional geographic locations;
* exercise code names;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/ops/names-nicka.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* simulated dates;
* obviously invented chains of command;
* accompanying planning documentation.

</div>

Evidence favouring genuine operations would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* independently verified vessel identities;
* matching maintenance or deployment records;
* corroborating personnel files;
* supporting operational orders;
* multiple independent copies of the same data across different systems.

</div>

None of that evidence is currently available.

McKinnon did not preserve the spreadsheet, could not remember the ship names, and no independently authenticated copy has ever emerged. As a result, investigators cannot perform the cross-checks that would normally distinguish simulation data from real operational records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/theguardian/2005/jul/09/weekend7.weekend2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Game over &#124; Gary Mc Kinnon &#124; The Guardian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Game over &#124; Gary Mc Kinnon &#124; The Guardian</span></span></span>

## The Balance of the Evidence

Viewed narrowly, the exercise hypothesis does not prove that McKinnon encountered training data. It does, however, provide a well-established conventional mechanism capable of explaining several features of his account simultaneously: unfamiliar ship names, transfer records, logistics spreadsheets and apparently realistic administrative documentation.

Because military exercises routinely generate convincing fictional paperwork, and because McKinnon himself acknowledged that the material could have been part of a game or hypothetical scenario, the exercise explanation remains one of the strongest non-extraordinary interpretations of the "fleet-to-fleet transfers". The decisive limitation is not the plausibility of either interpretation, but the absence of the original records needed to distinguish between them.
