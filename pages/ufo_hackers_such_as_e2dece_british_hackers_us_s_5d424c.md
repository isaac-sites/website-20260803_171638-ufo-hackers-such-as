From homes in Britain, intruders such as [Mathew Bevan]({{ 'mathew-bevan/' | relative_url }}) and Gary McKinnon searched American military and space networks because they believed the United States was the most likely custodian of hidden information about unidentified flying objects, advanced propulsion or secret technology. Their targets were thousands of miles away, yet poorly secured dial-up connections, internet gateways and reusable passwords made physical distance increasingly irrelevant.

The pattern mattered for reasons beyond UFO culture. ## The transatlantic hacker pattern

The first important episode involved Welsh hacker Mathew Bevan, known online as “Kuji”, and London teenager [Richard Pryce]({{ 'richard-pryce/' | relative_url }}), or “Datastream Cowboy”. During March and April 1994, more than 150 intrusions were made into the Rome Laboratory at Griffiss Air Force Base in New York. The laboratory conducted command, control, communications and intelligence research for the US Air Force, making it a more consequential target than an ordinary government website or public information service.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/aim96084.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Information Security: Computer Attacks at...22 May 1996 — During March and April 1994, more than 150...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1996</span></span></span>

The intruders used compromised accounts, password-capturing software and other penetrated machines to move through connected systems. The US Government Accountability Office reported that they installed “sniffers”, which record information travelling across a network, and “Trojan horses”, programs disguised or inserted to provide concealed access. They also routed activity through other computers and communications links, complicating efforts to determine where the intrusions originated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/aim96084.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Information Security: Computer Attacks at...22 May 1996 — During March and April 1994, more than 150...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1996</span></span></span>

This uncertainty initially made the episode appear more threatening than its eventual explanation. American investigators considered whether the activity represented foreign espionage rather than amateur exploration. The attackers’ ability to cross from one system into others illustrated a central weakness of the period: networks were trusted because they belonged to friendly or connected institutions, not necessarily because every user had been strongly authenticated. A person reaching one vulnerable machine could use it as a staging point from which to probe a wider defence environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/congress/1996_rpt/s104357.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource Programs104-357 the national information infrastructure protection...For example, the 1994 intrusion into the Rome...</span></span></span>

Bevan was subsequently associated with intrusions involving US Air Force, NASA and NATO systems. Contemporary reporting and later accounts describe him as motivated partly by a belief that American authorities possessed concealed UFO information. Pryce’s motivation appears to have been broader computer exploration rather than a clearly documented UFO investigation, so the pair should not simply be treated as identical “UFO hackers”. Their importance lies in the overlap: one investigation brought a UFO-motivated British intruder into contact with a younger British hacker whose activity had already penetrated a major US defence research network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cyber.tap.purdue.edu/blog/articles/hackers-of-the-90s/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cyber.tap.purdue.edu">[purdue.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cyber.tap.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hackers of the &#x27;90s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TAPAug 13, 2024 —... alien life and evidence of the existence of UFO&#x27;s. Gary McKinnon repeated the basic geography on a larger and more publicly visible scale. Between February 2001 and [March 2002]({{ 'march-2002/' | relative_url }}), according to the American extradition case, he gained [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) from a computer in London to 97 US government machines. The institutions named in official British records included the Army, Navy, Air Force, NASA and Department of Defense. McKinnon acknowledged entering American systems, although he contested important parts of the prosecution’s account of damage and malicious intent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — On 7 October 2004 the respondent government requested...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2004</span></span></span>

The similarities between Bevan and McKinnon are therefore more illuminating than the differences in scale. Both were British civilians rather than intelligence officers. Both were attracted to American systems by beliefs about UFO secrecy. Both reportedly exploited security failures that did not require physical presence in the United States. And in both cases, American authorities interpreted the breaches primarily through national-security and system-damage concerns, not through the intruders’ self-description as seekers of suppressed knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2005/jun/11/hacking.internetcrime" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## Why American systems held special appeal

For UFO-minded intruders, the United States appeared to offer an unusually concentrated set of desirable targets. Decades of UFO folklore had placed the US Air Force, the Pentagon, NASA and secretive defence contractors at the centre of stories about recovered craft, hidden aerospace programmes and altered imagery. An enthusiast who accepted those claims could conclude that the most valuable evidence would not be found in a British ministry but in American military or space-agency files.

Bevan’s interest was reportedly directed towards evidence of an American UFO cover-up. McKinnon later gave a more detailed account of the same underlying assumption: he said he searched NASA and military networks for material concerning UFOs, antigravity and allegedly suppressed energy technologies. He specifically linked his actions to claims that NASA personnel removed anomalous objects from space imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cyber.tap.purdue.edu/blog/articles/hackers-of-the-90s/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cyber.tap.purdue.edu">[purdue.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cyber.tap.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hackers of the &#x27;90s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TAPAug 13, 2024 —... alien life and evidence of the existence of UFO&#x27;s. Pryce was... hacking into NASA as a teenager. He later end...</span></span></span>

American defence systems also held practical appeal because they were numerous, interconnected and, in some cases, surprisingly weakly protected. Evidence given to a US Senate investigation noted that military “.mil” systems had a reputation among intruders for being easy to penetrate. McKinnon similarly described locating machines with weak or absent passwords rather than defeating a single, exceptionally sophisticated security barrier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/congress/1996_hr/s9606053.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program Security in Cyberspace III</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THE THREATIn fact, in the Rome Lab case [see Appendix B] the youthful hacker admitted he penetrated &quot;.mil&quot;13 sites because those sites we...</span></span></span>

That combination created a powerful feedback loop. UFO culture suggested that extraordinary material was hidden in American systems, while technical weakness made those systems appear reachable. Each successful login could then reinforce the belief that a larger secret lay just beyond the next account, server or directory. The target was not merely “America” in the abstract, but the networked institutional map associated with aerospace secrecy: air bases, laboratories, military branches, NASA facilities and defence-related contractors.

The resulting intrusions reveal a mismatch between the hackers’ imagined destination and the systems they actually encountered. They expected sealed digital vaults containing decisive evidence. In practice, they entered a sprawling mixture of research, administration, logistics and operational networks. A machine’s association with NASA or the US Air Force did not mean that it held classified UFO material. Yet unauthorised access could still disrupt ordinary defence work or expose credentials that opened routes into more sensitive systems.

The Rome Laboratory incident demonstrates this distinction particularly well. Its significance did not depend on whether Bevan found evidence relevant to UFOs. The laboratory was connected to a broad defence network, and the attackers obtained enough access to install monitoring tools and use compromised machines as platforms for further activity. The concrete security danger arose from control of networked computers, not from the truth of the intruders’ beliefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/aim96084.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Information Security: Computer Attacks at...22 May 1996 — During March and April 1994, more than 150...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1996</span></span></span>

McKinnon’s case carried the same tension. He portrayed his activity as research driven by [public-interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}) curiosity. US authorities alleged that operating-system files were deleted, that military networks were disrupted and that considerable recovery work was required. British parliamentary and governmental accounts record those allegations, but because McKinnon was never tried in the United States, contested questions about intent, damage and precise responsibility were not resolved through a completed criminal trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/latest-on-gary-mckinnon-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">latest on gary mckinnon case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">latest on gary mckinnon case</span></span></span>

The UFO claims themselves remain evidentially weak. McKinnon described seeing an unusual space image and references such as “Non-Terrestrial Officers”, but he did not preserve independently verifiable copies. Bevan’s reported search likewise produced no authenticated public release proving a concealed extraterrestrial programme. The lasting evidence is therefore about unauthorised access to American defence systems, not about the existence of the secrets being sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo hacker tells what he found</span></span></span>

## Distance no longer protected defence networks

These British cases appeared during a transition from dial-up computer culture to broadly connected internet systems. Earlier security planning often treated distance, specialist equipment and controlled premises as protective barriers. Network access weakened all three assumptions. A home computer and modem could place an overseas user at the electronic boundary of a military research facility without the intruder approaching its gates.

The Rome Laboratory attackers obscured their origins by passing through multiple systems and communications routes. Investigators had to distinguish the machine from which a connection appeared to arrive from the person actually controlling it. That problem was not incidental: compromised computers could be used both as targets and as stepping stones, producing trails that crossed national borders and institutional networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/aim96084.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Information Security: Computer Attacks at...22 May 1996 — During March and April 1994, more than 150...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1996</span></span></span>

At first, this could make an amateur intrusion resemble state activity. A person using a chain of foreign systems might appear to be an intelligence operative, while stolen information placed on an American computer could create misleading indications about who had collected it. The Rome Laboratory investigation became an early warning that attribution — determining who was really behind an intrusion — could be as difficult as closing the original security weakness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/congress/1996_rpt/s104357.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource Programs104-357 the national information infrastructure protection...For example, the 1994 intrusion into the Rome...</span></span></span>

McKinnon’s activity showed that the problem persisted into the next decade. The official allegation that one person in London accessed dozens of machines across several US institutions demonstrated how a distributed [government network]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}) could offer many entry points. Defending the most sensitive central system was insufficient when poorly managed peripheral computers, default credentials or trusted connections could provide an indirect route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — On 7 October 2004 the respondent government requested...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2004</span></span></span>

This is why the British identity of the intruders is historically significant. Britain was an American ally, not a conventional hostile power. The cases showed that cyber risk did not follow the diplomatic map: a connection originating in a friendly country could still be unauthorised, disruptive or difficult to attribute. Curiosity, subculture and personal belief could generate cross-border defence incidents without espionage orders or geopolitical conflict.

## Jurisdiction and enforcement problems

Once an intrusion crossed the Atlantic, investigators faced a basic question: which country should prosecute it? The suspect might be sitting in Britain, the affected computers might be spread across several American states, and much of the evidence might be held by US military agencies. Search powers, disclosure rules, admissibility standards and sentencing expectations differed between the two legal systems.

The Rome Laboratory cases produced sharply different outcomes from the severity initially attached to the intrusion. Richard Pryce pleaded guilty to 12 offences under the UK Computer Misuse Act and was fined £1,200. The prosecution of Mathew Bevan ended in 1997 when no evidence was offered and he was formally acquitted. Contemporary reporting said the court was told that he had not posed the national-security danger previously suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.independent.co.uk/news/court-frees-man-in-military-hacking-case-1295386.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: independent.co.uk">[independent.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">independent.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Independent Court frees man in military hacking case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Independent Court frees man in military hacking case</span></span></span>

Those outcomes did not mean that the network failures were trivial. They showed that a dramatic intelligence assessment and a sustainable criminal case are different things. Investigators may respond to an unexplained defence intrusion as a worst-case threat; prosecutors must later prove particular acts by a particular defendant under the relevant law.

The McKinnon dispute made the jurisdiction question a national political issue. The United States sought his extradition to face computer-crime charges, arguing that the affected systems, institutions and alleged damage were American. His supporters maintained that he had acted from Britain and could be prosecuted there, while also challenging the potential severity of American punishment and the pressures associated with plea bargaining. The House of Lords rejected his principal extradition arguments in 2008, but litigation and political review continued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — On 7 October 2004 the respondent government requested...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2004</span></span></span>

On 16 October 2012, Home Secretary Theresa May blocked extradition because medical evidence indicated that sending McKinnon to the United States would create such a high risk of suicide that extradition would be incompatible with his human rights. The decision did not declare the intrusions lawful or endorse his UFO claims; May explicitly described the alleged offences as serious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon extradition case home secretarys statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gary mckinnon extradition case home secretarys statement</span></span></span>

The case also strengthened pressure for a clearer “forum bar”, allowing courts to consider whether substantial conduct occurred in Britain and whether justice would be better served by a domestic trial. Parliamentary debate repeatedly used McKinnon as an example of the difficulty created when British authorities declined to prosecute but an overseas state sought extradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201011/cmhansrd/cm111205/debtext/111205-0003.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

Together, the Bevan, Pryce and McKinnon episodes show why early transatlantic hacking cases were so awkward to resolve. The internet allowed British intruders to reach American defence institutions, but criminal justice remained divided by national borders. US agencies experienced the intrusion and gathered much of the technical evidence; British police located the suspects and British courts controlled whether they would be tried locally or transferred abroad.

## The real legacy of the British connection

The memorable surface story is that British UFO enthusiasts entered American military and NASA computers in search of extraordinary secrets. The more important historical lesson is that belief-driven amateurs could expose genuine weaknesses in defence networks without possessing extraordinary technical resources or acting on behalf of a foreign government.

Their targets reflected the geography of modern UFO mythology: the United States was imagined as the centre of hidden aerospace knowledge. Their methods reflected the geography of the early internet, where location mattered less than connectivity, password discipline and trust between machines. Their legal cases, meanwhile, exposed a third geography — the stubborn territorial boundaries of courts, police powers and extradition law.

Nothing publicly authenticated from these intrusions proves an American extraterrestrial programme.         .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
