## Introduction

## McKinnon’s Description of the Image

McKinnon said his search was influenced by allegations that workers at Johnson Space Center maintained processed and unprocessed versions of space photographs, with anomalous objects supposedly removed from the processed copies. According to his 2006 account to *Wired*, he gained remote access to a NASA desktop and found folders containing very large, high-resolution images. His connection operated through a 56-kilobit-per-second dial-up service, making the transfer extremely slow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He claims to have discovered a NASA department that airbrushes UFO images from high-resolution photos and an Excel spreadsheet titled &quot;No...</span></span></span>

## Was the NASA Image Really a Spacecraft?

Nothing in the description alone permits a confident identification. “Cigar-shaped” is a broad visual category rather than a technical classification. Depending on viewing angle, cropping, resolution and contrast, ordinary objects such as spacecraft components, rocket stages, satellites, [reflections]({{ 'reflections/' | relative_url }}) or image artefacts can appear elongated and seamless.

The absence of scale is especially damaging. An object crossing a small part of a photograph might be a compact item near the camera, a conventional spacecraft at moderate distance or a much larger feature farther away. Without the image’s field of view, camera position, focal length, exposure, timestamp and target coordinates, apparent shape cannot establish physical dimensions. McKinnon himself acknowledged that there was no reference from which to estimate size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He claims to have discovered a NASA department that airbrushes UFO images from high-resolution photos and an Excel spreadsheet titled &quot;No...</span></span></span>

His viewing conditions also reduced the amount of recoverable information. Four-bit colour permits only 16 colours, while low screen resolution discards fine contours and surface detail. Those settings may have made a slow remote connection more usable, but they would also have amplified banding, simplified edges and concealed faint structures. A visually “smooth” surface under those conditions does not reliably show that the original object lacked joints, panels or other manufactured features.

Nor does the existence of processed and unprocessed image directories inherently indicate concealment. NASA formally distinguishes between raw instrument data and progressively processed products. Processing can include calibration, correction of sensor effects, geometric adjustment, contrast enhancement and conversion into scientifically interpretable imagery. The labels “raw”, “processed”, “filtered” or “unfiltered” therefore have routine technical meanings and do not by themselves demonstrate that objects were being erased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/learn/earth-observation-data-basics/data-processing-levels" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthdata.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthdata.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA EarthdataData Processing LevelsNASA data products are processed in levels ranging from Level 0 to Level 4. Level 0 products are raw...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that McKinnon saw an ordinary object. It means that the observation lacks the information required to choose among ordinary, artificial, erroneous or genuinely anomalous explanations. Declaring the object an extraterrestrial spacecraft would require evidence that excluded those alternatives, and the surviving account cannot do that.

## Possible Conventional Explanations

A conventional explanation cannot now be demonstrated, because the underlying image is unavailable. Several possibilities nevertheless fit the limited facts at least as well as the spacecraft interpretation.

**A known vehicle seen from an unfamiliar angle.** Spacecraft, payload adapters, upper rocket stages and cylindrical modules can appear cigar-shaped when their identifying features are obscured by distance or low resolution. Rounded tanks, antenna covers or attached components might resemble the “spheres” McKinnon remembered. Without a mission identifier or date, no comparison with a catalogue of [known objects]({{ 'known-objects/' | relative_url }}) is possible.

**A small object close to the camera.** Space imagery can contain debris, ice particles, insulation or equipment near a crewed vehicle. With no depth cues, a nearby object can appear to float over Earth while seeming far larger than it really is. A single still frame cannot disclose its distance or motion.

**A reflection or optical artefact.** Glare, internal lens reflections and out-of-focus foreground objects may produce smooth, symmetrical forms. NASA’s own published image notes sometimes identify lens artefacts and describe their removal during preparation for public or scientific use. Such correction is ordinary photographic practice, not evidence that a physical craft has been concealed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/great-balls-of-fire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Great Balls of Fire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Great Balls of Fire</span></span></span>

**A compression or display artefact.** Image compression can generate blocks, ringing, false boundaries and simplified shapes. NASA documentation explicitly notes that compressed spacecraft images may acquire a blocky or pixelated appearance, particularly after enhancement. Low-colour remote rendering would introduce a further layer of visual degradation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/image-compression/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Image Compression</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Image Compression</span></span></span>

**A partially loaded or misrendered image.** McKinnon was viewing the file through remote-control software while it was still transferring. If only part of the image had rendered, missing areas or progressive decoding could have altered the apparent outline. Without knowing the image format and software, it is impossible to reconstruct how the display behaved.

**An ordinary image interpreted through prior expectations.** McKinnon entered the system specifically looking for evidence of a UFO cover-up. That does not make his account dishonest, but prior expectations can shape how ambiguous visual material is classified. A person expecting to find a spacecraft is more likely to read an unfamiliar silhouette as one than someone approaching the same image without that expectation.

These explanations are possibilities, not solutions. Their importance is methodological: several ordinary mechanisms remain open, so an extraordinary identification cannot be reached from the description alone.

## Why the Missing File Matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

A preserved copy would have transformed the case. Investigators could have examined the pixels at full resolution, searched for editing boundaries, identified compression signatures and extracted metadata recording the camera, acquisition time, processing history and file origin. Earth features might have helped reconstruct the viewing geometry, while mission records could have shown which spacecraft or instruments were operating nearby.

</div>

A screenshot would have been weaker than the original file but still valuable. It could have fixed the object’s outline, apparent position and relationship to the Earth background. Independent analysts could then have tested whether later verbal descriptions matched what was actually visible. Instead, even the most basic characteristics depend on memory: the number and location of the spherical structures, the object’s orientation, the amount of Earth visible and the quality of its edges.

The lack of a file also prevents provenance analysis. There is no filename, directory path, catalogue number or timestamp linking the claimed image to a particular NASA programme. It is therefore impossible to determine whether it originated with a satellite, an astronaut camera, a simulation, a presentation, an engineering archive or some unrelated material stored on the same machine.

[Official records]({{ 'official-record/' | relative_url }}) do corroborate the broader setting only in a limited sense. The US Department of Justice alleged that McKinnon accessed and damaged military and NASA computers, while the House of Lords later recorded that the case involved 16 NASA computers among 97 US government systems. Neither source authenticates the cigar-shaped image or records it as material recovered during the investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Once inside a network, McKinnon would then use the hacked computer to find additional military and NASA victims...</span></span></span>

McKinnon said that police seized his hard drive after his arrest, but no publicly available forensic finding has established that it contained the image. His explanation was that the Java remote-access arrangement had not cached the picture locally and that he had failed to take a screenshot before being disconnected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He claims to have discovered a NASA department that airbrushes UFO images from high-resolution photos and an Excel spreadsheet titled &quot;No...</span></span></span>

That evidential gap cannot be repaired by repeating the description. Later articles, podcasts and social-media posts generally trace back to McKinnon’s interviews rather than to an independent second witness or recovered NASA record. Repetition may make the story feel corroborated, but sources copying the same original claim do not create additional evidence.

## A Careful Credibility Assessment

McKinnon’s account should not be dismissed merely because it concerns UFOs. It is possible for a witness to describe a real but unidentified image honestly and still be unable to prove what it showed. His description also contains cautionary details rather than complete certainty: he admitted that he had no scale and, when discussing related material, accepted that some of what he found might have an ordinary or hypothetical explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He claims to have discovered a NASA department that airbrushes UFO images from high-resolution photos and an Excel spreadsheet titled &quot;No...</span></span></span>

At the same time, sincerity is different from accuracy. The image was encountered during an unauthorised, technically constrained remote session by a viewer already convinced that NASA concealed UFO evidence. It was apparently seen briefly, at reduced colour depth and resolution, without contextual data. Those conditions create substantial risks of misperception and overinterpretation.

The most defensible classification is therefore **an unverified recollection of an unidentified image**. It is not proven false, because the missing file prevents a decisive conventional identification. It is not evidence of an extraterrestrial spacecraft, because the same absence prevents authentication, measurement and exclusion of ordinary alternatives.

Within the wider story of UFO-motivated hackers, the alleged NASA image illustrates the difference between gaining access to a sensitive network and obtaining reliable evidence from it. McKinnon’s access is documented; his interpretation of the image is not independently testable. The dramatic claim survives precisely where the usable data do not.
