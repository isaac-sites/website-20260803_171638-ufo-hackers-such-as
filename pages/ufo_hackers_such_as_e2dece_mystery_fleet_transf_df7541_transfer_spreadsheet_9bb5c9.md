## Introduction

Gary McKinnon consistently claimed that he discovered one or more spreadsheets containing references to personnel and transfers while browsing United States military computer systems in 2001–2002. However, no copy of the files, screenshots, filenames or metadata have ever been produced. As a result, the question is not what the spreadsheet *proved*, but what it *may have contained* based on McKinnon's own changing descriptions over two decades.

His various interviews suggest three possibilities: that he saw a single Excel workbook containing multiple worksheets; that he encountered several separate but related administrative spreadsheets; or that later recollections combined different files into one memory. Because the underlying documents no longer exist for independent examination, none of these interpretations can be confirmed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## How McKinnon Described the Workbook in Different Interviews

Although details shifted, several elements remained remarkably consistent.

Across multiple interviews McKinnon repeatedly recalled:

* a heading or workbook labelled **"Non-Terrestrial Officers"**;
* a relatively short list of names and ranks;
* transfer records involving ships;
* unfamiliar vessel names that he believed were absent from public naval lists; and
* no surviving copy of the spreadsheet itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Other details became more specific only in later retellings. For example, McKinnon eventually distinguished **"material" or "materiel" transfers** from personnel transfers, suggesting logistics records rather than merely staff assignments. In military usage, "materiel" refers to equipment and supplies rather than personnel, so if that recollection is accurate the workbook would have resembled an administrative logistics document more than a personnel roster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectcamelot.org/lang/en/gary_mckinnon_interview_transcript_en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectcamelot.org">[Project Camelot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectcamelot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on projectcamelot.org.</span></span></span>

Likewise, later interviews estimated approximately twenty to thirty officers and eight to ten ship names. Those figures do not appear in the earliest public accounts, making it impossible to know whether they reflect clearer memory or later reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projectcamelot.org/lang/en/gary_mckinnon_interview_transcript_en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projectcamelot.org">[Project Camelot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projectcamelot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on projectcamelot.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Linked Officers, Ships and Transfers Would Matter

If McKinnon's memory is substantially accurate, the significance lies less in any individual field than in how the information may have been connected.

An ordinary military workbook could plausibly contain several related worksheets such as:

Possible worksheetInformation McKinnon recalledPersonnelNames and ranksTransfersShip-to-ship or fleet-to-fleet movementsLogisticsMateriel or equipment transfersVessel referenceShip names or identifiers

A workbook organised in that way would represent a coherent administrative dataset rather than isolated notes. That internal consistency is one reason supporters have regarded his account as more compelling than a simple claim of seeing an unusual title.

However, there is an equally important limitation. None of the recalled fields included dates, command authorities, document classifications, unit identifiers, file paths or explanatory notes. Without those contextual elements, even an authentic spreadsheet would be difficult to interpret. Administrative databases routinely separate related information across multiple tables, abbreviate terminology and rely on internal reference codes that are meaningless outside their operational environment.

Consequently, even if the workbook genuinely linked officers, ships and transfers, its purpose could have ranged from routine logistics to planning exercises, training scenarios or classified operational administration. The surviving testimony alone cannot distinguish among those possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## What the Missing File Structure Prevents Us from Knowing

The absence of the original spreadsheet creates several important evidential gaps.

First, there is no way to determine whether "Non-Terrestrial Officers" was:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a workbook title;
* a worksheet name;
* a database export;
* a report heading; or
* simply one tab within a larger administrative file.

</div>

Second, no metadata survives. Investigators therefore cannot examine creation dates, authorship, revision history, directory location or linked files that might explain the spreadsheet's purpose.

Third, there is no opportunity to compare the terminology against contemporary military software. Words such as "transfer", "fleet", "officer" and even "non-terrestrial" could have had specialised meanings within a particular organisation that differed from ordinary usage.

Finally, the inability to inspect the workbook prevents testing whether McKinnon's recollections describe one integrated dataset or memories of several unrelated documents encountered during an extended search session. That distinction matters because a unified workbook connecting officers, ships and logistics would present a stronger internal narrative than disconnected administrative files viewed independently.

## The Evidence Supports Possibilities, Not Conclusions

The available evidence allows a cautious reconstruction of what McKinnon believed he saw, but not verification of what actually existed.

His accounts consistently point towards administrative records involving personnel, transfers and ship names. Over time he expanded those descriptions to include workbook tabs and materiel movements, making it plausible that he remembered either a multi-sheet Excel workbook or several closely related spreadsheets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.welivesecurity.com/2015/12/08/gary-mckinnon-reveals-detail-on-nasa-data-breach-and-extraterrestrial-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: welivesecurity.com">[WeLiveSecurity]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">welivesecurity.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on welivesecurity.com.</span></span></span>

What cannot be established is whether those files documented an ordinary military process, a [classified programme]({{ 'secret-projects/' | relative_url }}), a planning exercise or something else entirely.
