## Introduction

Gary McKinnon's successful resistance to extradition is sometimes presented as indirect proof that his claims about discovering hidden UFO-related information were credible. The legal record does not support that interpretation. The decisive UK government decision in 2012 addressed whether extraditing McKinnon to the United States would violate his [human rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}) because of an exceptionally high risk of suicide arising from his medical condition. It did not assess whether his claims about secret spacecraft, "non-terrestrial officers", or other alleged discoveries were true. Likewise, the later decision not to prosecute him in the United Kingdom reflected prosecutorial judgement about evidence, jurisdiction and the [public interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}) rather than any conclusion that his assertions about concealed UFO evidence had been substantiated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction is important within the broader debate over [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) versus proof of concealment. A court or government can conclude that extradition is inappropriate, or that a domestic prosecution is impractical, without expressing any view at all about the factual accuracy of the defendant's underlying beliefs.

## Why the Home Secretary blocked extradition

The central legal question before Home Secretary Theresa May was narrow. By the time the case reached her, the ordinary extradition process had already run its course. The remaining issue was whether extradition would be compatible with McKinnon's rights under the European Convention on Human Rights.

After reviewing extensive medical evidence, including independent expert advice, May concluded that extradition would create such a high risk that McKinnon would end his own life that ordering his surrender would be incompatible with his human rights. Her statement expressly described him as someone accused of serious offences while simultaneously recognising the severity of his medical condition, including Asperger's syndrome and depressive illness. She therefore withdrew the extradition order and referred the matter to the Director of Public Prosecutions to consider whether a UK prosecution should follow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

Nothing in that decision evaluated:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether McKinnon had found evidence of extraterrestrial technology;
* whether government agencies possessed hidden UFO files;
* whether his interpretation of files or images was correct; or
* whether his stated motive affected the truth of his allegations.

</div>

The decision was therefore a human-rights judgement rather than an evidential judgement about UFO claims.

## Why Britain later declined domestic prosecution

Following the extradition decision, the Crown Prosecution Service examined whether proceedings should instead take place in Britain. In 2012 the Director of Public Prosecutions concluded that there would be no UK prosecution.

That outcome has sometimes been misunderstood as official acceptance of McKinnon's account. In reality, prosecutors apply different tests from those used in political or scientific disputes. They consider whether there is sufficient admissible evidence, whether the alleged offences can realistically be prosecuted within the relevant [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}), and whether prosecution would be in the public interest.

In McKinnon's case, practical obstacles included the fact that much of the evidence, witnesses and affected computer systems were located in the United States. The alleged victims were primarily American military and NASA organisations, meaning that any British prosecution would have faced significant evidential and procedural difficulties. The CPS therefore decided that a realistic domestic prosecution was not appropriate despite the extradition being halted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because prosecutorial decisions often reflect available evidence and legal practicality rather than any endorsement or rejection of the accused person's personal beliefs.

## What neither decision said about UFO evidence

Neither the extradition decision nor the later CPS decision addressed the substance of McKinnon's extraordinary claims.

In particular, neither authority found that:

* secret "[free energy]({{ 'free-energy/' | relative_url }})" technology existed;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* classified spacecraft programmes had been identified;
* NASA had edited images to remove UFOs;
* lists of "non-terrestrial officers" represented evidence of extraterrestrial personnel; or
* government agencies had engaged in an organised UFO cover-up.

</div>

Those questions were simply outside the legal issues before the Home Secretary and prosecutors. Courts deciding extradition assess statutory requirements, human-rights obligations and procedural fairness. Prosecutors assess whether criminal charges can realistically be proved. Neither process functions as an investigation into scientific or historical claims about unidentified aerial phenomena.

The legal outcome therefore cannot reasonably be treated as indirect authentication of McKinnon's reported observations.

## Why the confusion persists

Several factors have encouraged misunderstanding.

First, the dramatic nature of the extradition battle received far greater public attention than the limited legal issues actually decided. Many reports focused on the emotional and political controversy surrounding extradition, making it easy for observers to assume that the entire dispute—including McKinnon's UFO allegations—had somehow been resolved.

Second, people sometimes assume that if governments abandon a prosecution or extradition request, they must have discovered weaknesses in the underlying allegations. Criminal justice systems do not work that way. Cases may end because of health concerns, evidential problems, jurisdictional limits, procedural barriers or public-interest considerations without changing the factual position on unrelated claims.

Finally, McKinnon's own account of what he believed he saw has continued to circulate independently of the legal record. The persistence of those stories does not alter what the official decisions actually established.
