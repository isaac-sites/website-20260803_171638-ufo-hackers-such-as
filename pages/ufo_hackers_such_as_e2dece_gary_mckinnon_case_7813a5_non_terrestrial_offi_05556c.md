## Introduction

The phrase **“non-terrestrial officers”** comes from a single claim made by British hacker Gary McKinnon about an Excel spreadsheet he said he viewed while unlawfully accessing United States military and NASA-related computer systems in 2001–2002. According to McKinnon, the spreadsheet listed names, ranks, ship names and “fleet-to-fleet transfers”, which he interpreted as evidence of a covert space-based military programme rather than extraterrestrial beings. The alleged spreadsheet has never been released, independently examined or authenticated. As a result, it remains one of the most frequently cited yet least verifiable elements of the McKinnon case. Understanding what the phrase could have meant requires separating McKinnon's recollections from what can actually be established from the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## Possible Military and Administrative Meanings

The wording "non-terrestrial officers" sounds extraordinary because it is unusual rather than because it has a recognised military definition. Without the original spreadsheet, several explanations remain possible.

One possibility is that the phrase referred to personnel assigned to space-based assets, satellite programmes or other organisations whose operational environment was technically outside the Earth's surface. Although no known US military personnel category officially uses the title "non-terrestrial officer", governments have long operated classified satellite, reconnaissance and space projects whose internal terminology is not publicly documented. The phrase could therefore have been an internal administrative label rather than evidence of an unknown fleet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelsheiserfoundation.org/articles/nasas-non-terrestrial-officers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelsheiserfoundation.org">[The Michael S. Heiser Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michaelsheiserfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Michael S. Heiser FoundationNASA’s Non-Terrestrial Officers? Debunking the Viral ConspiracyJanuary 25, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 25, 2016</span></span></span>

Another possibility is that the spreadsheet belonged to a planning exercise, simulation or research project. Military organisations routinely generate documents involving hypothetical units, future force structures and experimental concepts. McKinnon himself was asked whether the material might have represented a strategic planning exercise rather than operational records. While he rejected that explanation, he acknowledged he could not prove otherwise because he saw only a small portion of the file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

A more mundane explanation is that the terminology was simply being misunderstood outside its original organisational context. Isolated spreadsheet titles can appear far more dramatic when separated from surrounding documentation, project names or explanatory notes.

## Why the Wording Cannot Prove a Secret Space Fleet

The central problem is not whether the phrase is unusual—it clearly is—but whether the document can be independently evaluated. It cannot.

Several important limitations remain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **No original file exists publicly.** McKinnon never produced a copy of the spreadsheet, and no government agency has acknowledged such a document.
* **No screenshots have been authenticated.** Unlike some digital evidence cases, there is no preserved image showing the spreadsheet title or contents.
* **The claims rely on memory.** Public descriptions have been reconstructed from interviews conducted years after the events.
* **No corroborating witness has confirmed the file.** No administrator, investigator or independent researcher has verified seeing the same spreadsheet.

</div>

These evidential gaps mean that the spreadsheet cannot be treated as proof of any [classified programme]({{ 'secret-projects/' | relative_url }}), regardless of how intriguing the reported wording may appear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## The Ship Names and Transfer Records

McKinnon's account gave equal weight to another feature of the spreadsheet: references to ship names and transfers between vessels. He argued that because he could not match the names with publicly known naval ships, they might represent spacecraft rather than conventional naval assets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/theguardian/2005/jul/09/weekend7.weekend2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Game over &#124; Gary Mc Kinnon &#124; The Guardian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Game over &#124; Gary Mc Kinnon &#124; The Guardian</span></span></span>

However, that inference has significant limitations.

Military organisations often employ project names, hull designations, temporary identifiers, contractor names or internal abbreviations that never appear in public databases. Classified logistics systems may also reference platforms using identifiers that differ from publicly commissioned vessel names. Without the original spreadsheet or supporting documentation, there is no reliable way to determine whether the listed names referred to ships, databases, projects, software systems or something else entirely.

Consequently, the inability to recognise the names does not by itself establish that they belonged to an undisclosed space fleet.

## How the Claim Has Been Interpreted

Within UFO literature, the "non-terrestrial officers" spreadsheet became one of the principal foundations for later speculation about alleged covert space programmes, including narratives involving secret military fleets. Later writers often connected McKinnon's recollections with broader theories that were developed independently, despite there being no documentary evidence linking the spreadsheet to those claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theepochtimes.com/article/the-solar-warden-covert-space-project-fact-or-science-fiction-1253536" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theepochtimes.com">[The Epoch Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theepochtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Epoch TimesThe Solar Warden Covert Space Project: Fact or Science Fiction? &#124; The Epoch TimesFebruary 17, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 17, 2015</span></span></span>

Researchers taking a more sceptical approach generally emphasise a different point: the spreadsheet is interesting because of its reported title, but its evidential value is extremely limited. Since neither investigators nor independent analysts have been able to inspect the file itself, it cannot establish the existence of off-world military operations. At most, it represents an unverified recollection by the individual who viewed it during [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to government systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelsheiserfoundation.org/articles/nasas-non-terrestrial-officers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelsheiserfoundation.org">[The Michael S. ## What Can Be Said with Confidence

The "non-terrestrial officers" files occupy an unusual place in the Gary McKinnon story because they are simultaneously central to his UFO-related claims and almost impossible to evaluate.

The evidence supports only a few firm conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* McKinnon consistently stated that he saw an Excel spreadsheet titled **"Non-Terrestrial Officers."**
* He believed it listed personnel, ship names and transfer records consistent with a hidden space-based organisation.
* He interpreted "non-terrestrial" as meaning **not Earth-based**, not as referring to alien officers.
* No authenticated copy of the spreadsheet has ever been produced.
* Without the original file, ordinary administrative explanations and extraordinary interpretations remain possibilities, but neither can be confirmed from the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>
