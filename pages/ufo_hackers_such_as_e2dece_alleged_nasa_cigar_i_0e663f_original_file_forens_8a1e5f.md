## Introduction

If the original [NASA image]({{ 'nasa-image/' | relative_url }}) that Gary McKinnon said he briefly viewed were ever recovered in its original form, investigators would finally be able to move beyond competing recollections and apply standard digital and aerospace forensic methods. At present, no authenticated copy of the alleged image is publicly available, so neither supporters nor sceptics can perform meaningful technical analysis. A preserved original would not automatically prove that the image showed an unknown craft, but it could answer several concrete questions: when and where the image was acquired, whether it had been altered, what mission produced it, and whether the object could be matched to known spacecraft, hardware or imaging artefacts. These are routine investigative steps used in scientific image verification rather than UFO-specific techniques.

## Which Metadata Would Matter Most?

The first priority would be the file itself rather than the picture alone. Original NASA imagery often carries extensive metadata describing how and when it was acquired. Depending on the mission and file format, this can include acquisition time, instrument identifiers, spacecraft orientation, exposure settings, calibration information, processing history and links to the originating mission. NASA notes that archival scientific images preserve substantially richer metadata than publicly distributed preview versions, allowing researchers to reconstruct acquisition conditions with considerable precision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/multimedia/raw-images-faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Raw Images FAQ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceRaw Images FAQ - NASA ScienceNovember 2, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2, 2024</span></span></span>

## Could the Image Have Been Edited?

One of the recurring claims associated with McKinnon's account is that he allegedly encountered "processed" and "unprocessed" image folders. If an original file became available, digital forensic analysis could test whether the image had actually undergone editing rather than relying on anecdotal descriptions.

Investigators would examine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Compression history, including repeated JPEG recompression.
* Pixel noise consistency across the image.
* Edge transitions around the alleged object.
* Colour interpolation patterns.
* Clone detection indicating copied image regions.
* Evidence of layered editing or compositing.
* Thumbnail consistency where embedded previews exist.

</div>

Modern forensic techniques can often identify traces left by image manipulation, although they cannot always determine who performed an edit or why. Likewise, the absence of detectable editing would not prove that the image depicts an extraordinary object; it would merely indicate that no obvious digital alteration could be demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv A Forensic Methodology for Detecting Image Manipulations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Forensic Methodology for Detecting Image ManipulationsAugust 9, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 9, 2023</span></span></span>

It would also matter whether investigators obtained the highest-quality archival file rather than a compressed derivative. NASA distinguishes between archival scientific data—which preserve full resolution and bit depth—and compressed JPEG or PNG versions intended for public viewing. Compression can erase subtle forensic indicators and reduce the reliability of image analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/multimedia/raw-images-faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Raw Images FAQ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceRaw Images FAQ - NASA ScienceNovember 2, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2, 2024</span></span></span>

## Could NASA Records Identify the Scene?

A recovered image would not exist in isolation. NASA imagery is normally associated with mission documentation, engineering records and image catalogues.

Investigators could attempt to identify:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The spacecraft or platform that acquired the image.
* The mission timeline corresponding to the timestamp.
* Orbital position at the moment of acquisition.
* Camera pointing direction.
* Other frames captured immediately before and after.
* Mission logs describing scheduled observations.

</div>

Finding adjacent frames from the same image sequence would be especially valuable. If multiple photographs showed the same object moving consistently through successive frames, investigators could estimate its position and behaviour. Conversely, if neighbouring images revealed a conventional satellite, payload or camera artefact, the mystery might largely disappear.

NASA's archival collections preserve extensive historical imagery and associated documentation that can often be cross-referenced with mission records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/archives/hq-archives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives: Headquarters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Archives: Headquarters - NASAJuly 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Could Earth Features Reveal the Location?

McKinnon recalled seeing Earth beneath the object but could not identify the location or estimate the object's size. An original high-resolution file might allow investigators to determine both.

Analysts could compare visible geographical features with known Earth imagery to identify:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Coastlines.
* Cloud formations.
* Ice fields.
* Ocean colour.
* Mountain ranges.
* Night-time city lights, if applicable.

</div>

Once the Earth's location in the frame had been established, orbital mechanics could narrow the range of spacecraft capable of acquiring the image at that time. If the observing platform were identified, the apparent size and distance of the object could be estimated far more accurately than from memory alone.

Scale is particularly important because many objects photographed in orbit appear visually ambiguous without distance information. An item close to the camera can resemble a much larger object hundreds of kilometres away.

## Could the Object Match Known Space Hardware?

The most practical question would be whether the object matched documented aerospace hardware.

Investigators would compare its shape against:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Satellites operating during the relevant period.
* Shuttle payloads.
* International Space Station components.
* Rocket stages.
* Space debris catalogues.
* Calibration targets or engineering hardware.

</div>

Lighting geometry would also be examined. [Reflections]({{ 'reflections/' | relative_url }}) from cylindrical spacecraft, insulation blankets or fuel tanks can create smooth, featureless silhouettes that differ substantially from their physical appearance. A single viewing angle may hide structural details such as solar panels or antennas.

If the object's position and acquisition time were known, orbital catalogues maintained by space-tracking organisations could determine whether a known object occupied the same location.

## What Could the File Actually Prove?

Even an authenticated original would have evidential limits.

It could potentially establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* That the image genuinely originated within a NASA imaging system.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://image.gsfc.nasa.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: image.gsfc.nasa.gov">[image.gsfc.nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">image.gsfc.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* Whether it had been digitally modified.
* When and how it was acquired.
* Which mission produced it.
* Whether the depicted object corresponded to known aerospace hardware.

</div>

It could not, by itself, demonstrate the existence of extraterrestrial technology. An unexplained object in a single authenticated photograph would remain only that: unexplained until supported by independent evidence such as additional images, telemetry, mission documentation or corroborating observations.

This distinction is important in the McKinnon case. The absence of the original file prevents every one of these technical tests. As a result, the alleged cigar-shaped NASA image remains an unverified personal recollection rather than a piece of evidence that can be independently examined using established digital forensic and aerospace investigative methods.
