## Introduction

Gary McKinnon's account of finding evidence for concealed UFO-related technology ultimately depends on material that nobody else has been able to examine. He has consistently said that he briefly viewed what appeared to be an unusual spacecraft image and spreadsheets referring to "Non-Terrestrial Officers", but he also says he failed to preserve copies before his connection was terminated. That missing evidence is not a minor detail—it is the central reason his claims remain unverified. His sincerity, legal ordeal and technical ability to gain [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) do not solve the core evidential problem: independent investigators cannot inspect the files he described, determine their origin, or test alternative explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Within the broader discussion of alleged suppressed propulsion or energy technologies, this missing evidential chain is the dividing line between an intriguing personal testimony and a claim that can be independently evaluated.

## Why the absence of the files changes the assessment

The strongest evidence in any digital investigation is normally the original data or a demonstrably authentic copy. Without that, every subsequent discussion relies on recollection rather than examination.

## What McKinnon did not preserve

McKinnon has repeatedly explained why he believes no evidence survived. According to his account, he was connected through a slow 56 kbps dial-up connection while remotely controlling a NASA computer. He says that as a large image slowly rendered on screen, administrators detected the intrusion and disconnected the session before he could save it.

He has also stated that the Java-based remote viewing software did not leave him with a usable downloaded image or screenshot before the connection ended. In later interviews he similarly acknowledged that he had no copy of either the alleged spacecraft image or the spreadsheet listing "Non-Terrestrial Officers".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Because of this, there is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* no original image file;
* no preserved screenshot;
* no recovered temporary file;
* no spreadsheet copy;
* no associated metadata;
* no cryptographic hash proving authenticity; and
* no archived directory listings showing the surrounding context.

</div>

The entire evidential record therefore consists of McKinnon's own description of what he believes he saw.

## How memory and context can reshape recollection

Human memory is reconstructive rather than photographic. People generally remember the meaning of an event more reliably than every visual or technical detail.

This matters because McKinnon entered the systems with an existing expectation that hidden UFO programmes and advanced propulsion technology existed. By his own account, he had been influenced by disclosure advocates and believed NASA maintained original and edited image collections. That prior expectation does not invalidate his testimony, but it means later recollections cannot be treated as objective reproductions of what appeared on screen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Context is equally important in computing environments. File names, database fields and directory labels often use shorthand understood only by the people who created them. Without surrounding documentation, a label such as "Non-Terrestrial Officers" cannot be confidently interpreted. It might support an extraordinary claim, but it could also refer to something entirely conventional, such as personnel assigned outside Earth's surface, simulation data or an internal naming convention. Because the original file has never been examined, these possibilities cannot be resolved.

## What forensic material would have allowed verification

The debate would look very different if even a small amount of verifiable digital evidence had survived.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the original image file with embedded metadata;
* a screenshot captured during the session;
* cached fragments recoverable from the client computer;
* directory listings showing surrounding files;
* timestamps and file paths;
* server logs identifying the accessed resources; or
* authenticated copies preserved through established forensic procedures.

</div>

Digital forensic practice places considerable importance on preserving original files together with metadata and maintaining a documented [chain of custody]({{ 'chain-of-custody/' | relative_url }}) so that others can verify authenticity and reproduce findings. Without those elements, investigators are largely limited to witness testimony rather than technical analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-investigations-digital-imaging-and-multimedia-procedure/digital-imaging-and-multimedia-procedure-v30" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digital Imaging and Multimedia Procedure v3.0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital Imaging and Multimedia Procedure v3.0</span></span></span>

## Why later investigations cannot fill the gap

One common misunderstanding is that later investigators could simply retrieve the same material.

In reality, there is no public evidence identifying the precise systems, folders or files McKinnon claimed to have viewed. Even if backups once existed, there is no publicly available chain linking any surviving records to his specific observations. Organisations also routinely modify storage systems, migrate archives and remove obsolete files over time.

As a result, no independent researcher has produced authenticated copies matching McKinnon's descriptions despite many years of [public interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}). The absence of corroborating material does not demonstrate that the files never existed, but it leaves the claims without independent technical support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## What this means for claims of suppressed technology

The missing files are especially significant because the broader claim concerns extraordinary technological capabilities, including alleged antigravity propulsion and suppressed energy systems. Such claims require unusually strong evidence.

McKinnon's account remains valuable as a statement of what motivated his search and what he believes he encountered. It also explains why the story continues to attract attention within UFO disclosure discussions. However, the evidential status of the claim is limited by the absence of material that can be independently examined.

The distinction is therefore straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **McKinnon's testimony** documents what he says he observed.
* **Missing digital evidence** prevents others from testing whether those observations were accurate.
* **No authenticated files** means the alleged technology cannot be evaluated on forensic grounds.

</div>

That evidential gap—not his credibility alone—is why the claimed discovery of suppressed UFO-related technology remains unresolved rather than established.
