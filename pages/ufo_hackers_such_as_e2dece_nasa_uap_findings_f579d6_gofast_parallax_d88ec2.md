## Introduction

NASA’s discussion of the US Navy’s **GoFast** video did not conclude that the object had been positively identified. Instead, it showed that the footage itself does **not** support one of the video's most widely repeated claims—that it depicts an object racing just above the ocean at extraordinary speed. By analysing the aircraft's displayed telemetry, viewing geometry and the effects of parallax, NASA concluded that the object could have been much higher than it appeared and travelling at a speed consistent with an ordinary object drifting with upper-level winds. The case has therefore become a textbook example of why compelling imagery alone is not enough to establish extraordinary performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

Within the broader discussion of NASA's findings on UFO evidence and data quality, the GoFast analysis illustrates the agency's central message: reliable conclusions depend on accurate geometry, calibrated measurements and complete sensor data, not on visual impressions.

## What viewers see in the footage

The GoFast video, recorded by aviators from the USS *Theodore Roosevelt* strike group, appears at first glance to show a small object racing across the ocean surface. The infrared targeting camera follows the object while the aircraft moves at high speed, giving the impression that the target is skimming only a short distance above the water.

That visual impression became one of the reasons the video attracted so much attention after its public release. To many viewers, the apparent speed seemed difficult to reconcile with conventional aircraft or balloons.

NASA's review, however, emphasised that the human eye is a poor judge of an object's true motion when the observer is also moving rapidly. The video contains numerical readouts—including aircraft altitude, camera elevation angle, target range and aircraft speed—that allow a more rigorous analysis than visual inspection alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

## How altitude and parallax change the picture

NASA's calculation begins with straightforward geometry rather than speculation about what the object might be.

Using the aircraft's altitude, the camera's downward viewing angle and the measured range displayed on the targeting system, the study estimated that the object was approximately **13,000 feet above sea level**, not just above the waves as many viewers assumed. At that altitude, the object would have been roughly **4.2 miles beyond the point where the camera's line of sight intersected the ocean**, creating a misleading visual relationship with the sea surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

This is where **parallax** becomes crucial.

Parallax is the apparent movement of an object caused by a changing viewpoint. Because the F/A-18 was travelling at roughly **435 mph**, even a relatively slow-moving distant object would appear to sweep rapidly across the background as the aircraft passed. The apparent speed seen on screen therefore combines:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the object's own motion;
* the jet's high forward speed;
* the changing viewing angle as the aircraft manoeuvred; and
* perspective effects created by the camera's line of sight.

</div>

NASA argued that these combined effects can easily create the illusion of exceptional velocity without requiring the object itself to be moving unusually fast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

## What NASA's calculation found

NASA then examined how the target's position changed over approximately **22 seconds** of footage.

Using the aircraft's changing heading, measured range and bearing to the object, the study estimated that the target travelled roughly **390 metres** during that period. That corresponds to an average speed of approximately **40 mph (about 64 km/h)**.

Importantly, NASA did **not** present this figure as an exact measurement. Instead, it described it as an estimate subject to uncertainty because the available data were incomplete and factors such as wind acting on the aircraft were simplified in the calculation.

Nevertheless, the analysis demonstrated that the available measurements did **not** require an object travelling at extraordinary speed. A velocity comparable to typical wind speeds at around 13,000 feet remained entirely consistent with the observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

## What the infrared image could—and could not—show

NASA also considered the thermal appearance of the target.

In the selected infrared display mode, the object appeared colder than the ocean background rather than showing evidence of a hot propulsion source. While infrared imagery alone cannot identify an object, the display did not reveal obvious thermal signatures expected from a powerful propulsion system.

NASA therefore suggested that a wind-driven object represented a plausible explanation compatible with both the estimated speed and the thermal data. At the same time, the report stressed that the available information was insufficient to determine the object's exact identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

## What the calculation cannot prove

One of the most common misunderstandings is to treat NASA's analysis as proving that the object was a balloon or proving that the case was completely solved.

The report does neither.

Instead, it establishes narrower conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* The video does **not** demonstrate that the object was flying just above the ocean.
* The displayed telemetry allows a conventional geometric explanation for the apparent high speed.
* The estimated motion is compatible with ordinary atmospheric drift.

## Later assessments and remaining debate

NASA's treatment of GoFast has been broadly echoed by the US Department of Defense's **All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)**. ## Why GoFast became a lesson in data quality

The lasting significance of the GoFast case is methodological rather than sensational.

It demonstrates how a striking video can create a powerful intuitive impression that changes substantially once instrument readings, geometry and measurement uncertainty are taken into account. Rather than resolving every mystery, NASA used the case to illustrate a broader scientific principle: conclusions about unidentified aerial phenomena should be based on complete, calibrated datasets instead of visual appearance alone.

That lesson aligns directly with NASA's wider assessment of UAP evidence. Strong claims require strong measurements, and when key data are missing, the scientifically responsible conclusion is often not that something extraordinary occurred, but that the available evidence cannot support that inference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6953219e33090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
