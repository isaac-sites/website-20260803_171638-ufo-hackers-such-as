## Introduction

The damage question sits at the centre of the [Gary McKinnon case]({{ 'mc-kinnon-case/' | relative_url }}). ### Operational harm, not physical destruction

In computer-crime law, “damage” does not require smashed equipment or permanently destroyed hardware. Loss of availability, deletion of accounts, restoration work and compromised system integrity can all qualify. That distinction mattered in McKinnon’s case because the alleged harm largely consisted of systems becoming unavailable or unreliable and technicians having to restore them.

It also explains why McKinnon’s use of a home computer and dial-up connection did not automatically rule out substantial consequences. A remote intruder does not need high bandwidth to delete a small but essential configuration file, alter permissions or disable accounts. The amount of data transmitted can be modest while the recovery work is extensive. Whether McKinnon performed the specific destructive acts alleged remained disputed, but the technical category of harm described by prosecutors was plausible in principle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Justice British National Charged with Hacking Into N.J</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">this intrusion into the network, McKinnon allegedly caused approximately $290,431 in damage to NWS Earle by deleting comp...</span></span></span>

## How damage increased the legal stakes

The alleged damage transformed the case from a story about trespass into one about interference with military operations. Accessing an unclassified machine without permission was already criminal conduct, but allegations of disabling networks after 11 September 2001 gave prosecutors a much stronger public-safety narrative. Naval Weapons Station Earle supplied munitions to the Atlantic Fleet, while the Military District of Washington had responsibilities connected with the US capital. Framing the intrusions as operational disruption made the place and timing of the conduct central to the prosecution’s seriousness assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Justice British National Charged with Hacking Into N.J</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">this intrusion into the network, McKinnon allegedly caused approximately $290,431 in damage to NWS Earle by deleting comp...</span></span></span>

Financial loss also mattered under US computer-crime charges because demonstrable damage can affect charging, [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}) and sentencing. The indictments therefore quantified losses and connected them to individual computers or facilities. In the New Jersey case, the alleged loss of approximately $290,431 made the claimed impact concrete; in the broader extradition case, the figure above $700,000 conveyed a campaign of accumulated harm rather than a series of harmless visits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Justice British National Charged with Hacking Into N.J</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">this intrusion into the network, McKinnon allegedly caused approximately $290,431 in damage to NWS Earle by deleting comp...</span></span></span>

The prosecution’s language additionally challenged McKinnon’s preferred public identity. A “searcher” might read documents and leave; a saboteur impairs systems, destroys records or obstructs recovery. By alleging deleted logs, inaccessible accounts and prolonged outages, the US authorities placed his conduct on the second side of that divide. McKinnon’s supporters, by contrast, stressed his lack of espionage, financial or terrorist motive and argued that the case was being amplified to punish an embarrassing exposure of weak military security. Contemporary US officials acknowledged that there was no evidence he supplied information to a foreign government or terrorist organisation, although absence of espionage did not answer the separate accusation of computer damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2002/11/brit-fights-hacking-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">attempts to extradite him for charges related to what is described as the largest successful hacking effort against American military net...</span></span></span>

## What the extradition judgments did — and did not — establish

British judgments are often cited as though they conclusively proved that McKinnon deleted the files and caused every alleged outage. Their actual function was more limited. Extradition courts were deciding whether the legal requirements for surrender to the United States had been met, not conducting the American criminal trial in Britain.

The House of Lords gave a detailed summary of the alleged conduct because the seriousness of the offences, McKinnon’s admissions and the proposed US proceedings were relevant to his extradition appeal. Its judgment stated that forensic analysis of his home computer confirmed the allegations, but the court did not hear the full prosecution and defence evidence needed for a criminal verdict. The summary was based on the case advanced by the requesting US authorities and on material used within the extradition process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — It damaged computers by impairing their integrity, The...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because extradition proceedings generally test whether a foreign prosecution is legally valid and sufficiently supported under the applicable treaty arrangements. They do not normally require the requesting state to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. McKinnon could challenge matters such as [human rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}), abuse of process and the consequences of extradition, but he did not receive a completed evidential trial in which technical witnesses were cross-examined about each deletion, outage and cost calculation.

It would therefore be misleading to say that British courts found him guilty of causing $700,000 in damage. It would be equally misleading to say that the allegations were invented or unsupported merely because there was no verdict. They were formal, particularised charges backed by investigative material sufficient to sustain a long-running extradition request. Their ultimate accuracy remained unresolved.

## Why no completed trial matters

The extradition process ended in October 2012 when Home Secretary Theresa May concluded that sending McKinnon to the United States would create such a high risk of suicide that extradition would be incompatible with his human rights. Her decision did not declare him innocent and did not reject the American damage evidence. She explicitly characterised the accusations as serious while deciding that his health made extradition impermissible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon extradition case home secretarys statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gary mckinnon extradition case home secretarys statement</span></span></span>

The possibility of a British prosecution was then considered. In December 2012, the Crown Prosecution Service decided not to charge him in England and Wales. The stated obstacles included the passage of time, the location of most witnesses and physical evidence in the United States, the sensitivity and volume of unused material, and the difficulty of transferring an American-built case into a British trial while meeting disclosure obligations. The CPS concluded that the prospect of securing a conviction reflecting the full extent of the alleged criminality was not high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/dec/14/gary-mckinnon-no-uk-charges" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon no uk charges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gary mckinnon no uk charges</span></span></span>

That decision is sometimes interpreted as a rejection of the damage claims. It was not. The CPS said that the evidential geography of the case still favoured the United States: most witnesses, physical evidence and supporting material remained there. Its conclusion concerned whether a viable prosecution could be mounted in England and Wales a decade later, not whether no damage had occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/dec/14/gary-mckinnon-no-uk-charges" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon no uk charges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gary mckinnon no uk charges</span></span></span>

The absence of a completed trial left several questions unanswered:

* Which deleted files and accounts could be forensically attributed to McKinnon rather than to administrators, automated processes or unrelated intruders?

## Searcher or saboteur?

The strongest evidence for the “searcher” interpretation is McKinnon’s acknowledged UFO-related motive, the absence of an alleged profit or espionage scheme and his consistent denial that he intended to damage systems. His methods also exposed embarrassingly weak security on some government machines, including weak or missing passwords.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The search for proof of the existence of UFOs landed Gary McKinnon in a world of trouble.... Save this story... McKinnon tells what he...</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence for the “saboteur” interpretation is the specificity of the US allegations: nine computers stripped of critical files, thousands of deleted accounts, erased naval logs, disabled networks and substantial restoration costs. The case was not framed simply as an administrator charging for password resets after an intrusion. Prosecutors claimed identifiable operational failures at named military facilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — It damaged computers by impairing their integrity, The...</span></span></span>

Neither label is fully satisfactory as a substitute for adjudicated facts. McKinnon was unquestionably an unauthorised intruder, not a lawful researcher. Yet “saboteur” implies a destructive purpose that he denied and that no completed trial established. A fair description is that he presented himself as a UFO-motivated searcher whose intrusions US prosecutors alleged became materially destructive.

That unresolved tension explains much of the case’s cultural afterlife. For admirers, the damage claims appeared to turn an eccentric hunt for hidden information into an exaggerated national-security prosecution. For US authorities, the UFO explanation did not neutralise deleted records, unavailable systems or the burden of restoring compromised defence networks. The legal system never delivered the adversarial hearing that might have separated those narratives more decisively.

## The governance lesson behind the dispute

The damage controversy exposed two governance failures that can exist at the same time. First, a remote user was able to enter numerous defence and space-agency systems through elementary security weaknesses. Military organisations had an obligation to enforce strong credentials, monitor remote access and isolate sensitive functions. The intrusions demonstrated serious deficiencies regardless of McKinnon’s motives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2002/11/brit-fights-hacking-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">attempts to extradite him for charges related to what is described as the largest successful hacking effort against American military net...</span></span></span>

Second, weak security does not grant outsiders permission to explore [government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}). Once inside, an intruder may unintentionally interrupt services, alter timestamps, overwrite evidence or trigger expensive incident-response procedures. Curiosity-driven access can therefore produce real operational costs even without a plan to steal secrets or destroy infrastructure.

The case also illustrates why public damage claims in cyber prosecutions need transparent, technically grounded accounting. Investigation costs, emergency recovery, software reinstallation, lost staff time and long-term security improvements are not interchangeable. Separating them helps courts assess causation and proportionality while protecting defendants from inflated headline totals. McKinnon’s case never reached the stage where those categories could be publicly tested.

The most defensible conclusion is consequently narrower than either side’s preferred story. US authorities advanced a substantial and detailed case that McKinnon’s intrusions disrupted military systems and generated hundreds of thousands of dollars in losses. McKinnon admitted unauthorised access but denied knowingly destructive conduct. Because extradition was stopped on human-rights grounds and no British prosecution followed, the central damage allegations remain serious, documented and legally consequential — but not finally proved by a criminal verdict.
