## Introduction

The final day of the intrusion period alleged against Gary McKinnon was **19 March 2002**, not because investigators observed another unauthorised login, but because the investigation moved from covert technical tracing to overt law enforcement. Following a United States request for mutual legal assistance, British authorities seized McKinnon's home computer in London and interviewed him under caution. That intervention effectively ended the period during which US prosecutors alleged he had access to American military and NASA systems and marked the beginning of a decade-long legal battle over extradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

## Why 19 March became the cutoff date

The date of **19 March 2002** appears consistently throughout the subsequent legal proceedings because it represents the point at which investigators acted on evidence already gathered rather than continuing to monitor the alleged intrusions. The House of Lords, when later reviewing McKinnon's extradition case, recorded that the United States alleged [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) between **1 February 2001 and 19 March 2002**. It also noted that, in March 2002, the United States submitted requests to the United Kingdom for mutual legal assistance, leading directly to the seizure of McKinnon's home computer and interviews under caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

By this stage, investigators had apparently moved beyond identifying suspicious network activity. The case had become an international criminal investigation requiring cooperation between American agencies and British authorities. Rather than simply tracing connections across networks, law enforcement sought physical evidence that could link the activity to a specific individual and computer.

This transition is significant because it separates two phases of the case:

* **Technical investigation**, focused on identifying the source of unauthorised connections.
* **Criminal investigation**, focused on preserving evidence, interviewing the suspect and preparing possible prosecution in the United States.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

## The seizure of McKinnon's home computer

The execution of the mutual legal assistance request resulted in British investigators seizing McKinnon's computer equipment on **19 March 2002**. According to later judicial summaries, forensic examination of those computers became one of the central pieces of evidence relied upon during the extradition proceedings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vlex.co.uk/vid/mckinnon-v-united-states-793612009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vlex.co.uk">[vLex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vlex.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">v Lex Mc Kinnon v United States of America</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McKinnon v United States of America - vLex United KingdomApril 3, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2007</span></span></span>

Court summaries describe investigators as finding material that prosecutors argued connected McKinnon's computer directly to the compromised US systems. Among the items cited were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* administrative usernames and passwords corresponding to numerous compromised computers;
* copies of the remote administration software **RemotelyAnywhere** matching versions installed on many affected systems;
* evidence that the software had allegedly been installed in distinctive directory locations chosen by McKinnon;
* documents describing methods for conducting the intrusions; and
* indications that the seized computer itself had not been remotely controlled by another unknown attacker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vlex.co.uk/vid/mckinnon-v-united-states-793612009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vlex.co.uk">[vLex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vlex.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">v Lex Mc Kinnon v United States of America</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McKinnon v United States of America - vLex United KingdomApril 3, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2007</span></span></span>

</div>

Whether every forensic interpretation would have survived a contested criminal trial was never tested, because no trial ultimately took place. Nevertheless, these findings formed a substantial part of the evidential basis presented in the extradition litigation.

## Interviews under caution and admissions

British authorities interviewed McKinnon under caution on **19 March 2002**, with a second interview taking place on **8 August 2002**. According to later court judgments summarising the prosecution case, he admitted responsibility for gaining unauthorised access to US government computers, installing remote-control software on compromised machines and copying files from American systems. He also acknowledged deleting log files in an attempt to conceal his activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vlex.co.uk/vid/mckinnon-v-united-states-793612009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vlex.co.uk">[vLex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vlex.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">v Lex Mc Kinnon v United States of America</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McKinnon v United States of America - vLex United KingdomApril 3, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 3, 2007</span></span></span>

Those interviews became important because they shifted the dispute away from identifying the person behind the intrusions. Instead, later legal arguments increasingly centred on different questions:

* the scale of the alleged damage;
* McKinnon's motivations, including his stated search for UFO-related information;
* the appropriate [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}) for prosecution; and

## From network investigation to extradition case

The March 2002 intervention effectively closed the operational phase of the investigation. Once McKinnon's equipment had been seized, investigators no longer needed to rely primarily on network traces and system logs. Instead, they possessed physical evidence and interview records that could support formal criminal proceedings.

The investigation then followed a series of legal milestones:

* US grand juries returned indictments in **October and November 2002**.
* American prosecutors formally pursued extradition from the United Kingdom.
* The case evolved into a prolonged legal and political dispute over extradition, proportionality and [mental health]({{ 'mental-health/' | relative_url }}), rather than continuing as an active cyber intrusion investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

## The significance of March 2002 in the intrusion timeline

Within the overall chronology of McKinnon's alleged intrusions, **19 March 2002** serves as the clear dividing line between alleged unauthorised access and the legal process that followed. It was not merely the last recorded date in the indictment period; it was the point at which investigators converted months of technical investigation into evidential action through international legal cooperation.

From that day onward, the central questions were no longer how access had been obtained or whether investigators could identify the source.
