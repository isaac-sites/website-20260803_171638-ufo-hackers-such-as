## Introduction

Gary McKinnon, [Mathew Bevan]({{ 'mathew-bevan/' | relative_url }}) and [Richard Pryce]({{ 'richard-pryce/' | relative_url }}) are frequently grouped together as Britain's "UFO hackers", creating the impression that they formed a coordinated group pursuing hidden government knowledge. The historical record does not support that picture. While the three men shared an interest in computer intrusion and, to varying degrees, curiosity about official secrecy, they belonged to different periods, faced different investigations and operated under different circumstances. The only genuine overlap was between Bevan and Pryce during the 1994 hacking scene. McKinnon's better-known campaign occurred seven to eight years later, with no credible evidence that he was recruited, directed or technically assisted by either of the earlier hackers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmhansrd/cm090715/debtext/90715-0012.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Commons Hansard Debates for 15 July 2009 (pt 0012)July 15, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2009</span></span></span>

## Bevan and Pryce shared a scene, not a long-term organisation

Mathew Bevan ("Kuji") and Richard Pryce ("Datastream Cowboy") were active during the same period in the mid-1990s and moved within the same British hacking community. Their cases became linked because investigators found that intrusions into the US Air Force's [Rome Laboratory]({{ 'rome-laboratory/' | relative_url }}) and related systems involved both individuals. They exchanged knowledge within that broader hacking milieu and some compromised systems were used as stepping stones into other networks.

This overlap, however, should not be mistaken for a formal team. Contemporary investigations treated them as individuals involved in the same wave of intrusions rather than members of an organised group with a continuing command structure or shared long-term objectives. Their [legal outcomes]({{ 'legal-outcomes/' | relative_url }}) also diverged:

* Richard Pryce was prosecuted in the United Kingdom under the Computer Misuse Act and fined.
* Charges against Mathew Bevan were eventually abandoned when the Crown Prosecution Service concluded that continuing the prosecution was no longer in the [public interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmhansrd/cm090715/debtext/90715-0012.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Commons Hansard Debates for 15 July 2009 (pt 0012)July 15, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2009</span></span></span>

Even Bevan's later comments about searching for UFO information describe his own motivation rather than a coordinated mission with Pryce or anyone else. Parliamentary discussions comparing his case with McKinnon's similarly treated them as separate prosecutions sharing some thematic similarities rather than participants in a single conspiracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmhansrd/cm090715/debtext/90715-0012.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Commons Hansard Debates for 15 July 2009 (pt 0012)July 15, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2009</span></span></span>

## McKinnon's campaign happened years later and alone

Gary McKinnon's intrusions took place during 2001 and 2002, long after the Rome Laboratory incident had become a landmark cyber-security case. By then the internet, investigative methods and legal treatment of computer crime had changed substantially.

McKinnon consistently presented himself as acting alone. Using the online name "Solo", he said he searched military and NASA systems for evidence relating to UFOs, advanced propulsion and alleged suppression of technology. United States prosecutors likewise described him as a lone intruder rather than part of a wider British hacking network. Contemporary reporting on his indictment stated that investigators believed he had acted alone and had no connection to any organised group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/2002/11/13/briton-indicted-as-hacker/7e4a2ad4-caab-4f9a-a71c-ddbcdd192992/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post Briton Indicted as Hacker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostBriton Indicted as Hacker - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>

Equally significant is what investigators did **not** allege. During the lengthy extradition proceedings:

* no indictment linked McKinnon to Bevan or Pryce;
* no court identified a conspiracy involving all three men;
* no official investigation alleged shared planning, recruitment or technical support between their cases.

Given the scale of the American investigation into McKinnon's activities, such links would likely have featured prominently had credible evidence existed.

## Similar interests did not make them collaborators

One reason the three cases are often merged is that both Bevan and McKinnon expressed interest in UFO-related material. That common theme creates an appealing narrative, but similar motives are not evidence of cooperation.

Their activities differed in important ways:

* **Timing:** Bevan and Pryce were active in 1994, while McKinnon's intrusions occurred between 2001 and 2002.
* **Operational context:** Bevan and Pryce emerged from the early internet hacking culture centred on vulnerable academic and military networks, whereas McKinnon operated years later from his own home using widespread internet connectivity.
* **Investigative record:** The earlier case focused on a network intrusion campaign centred on Rome Laboratory; the later case centred on McKinnon's alleged access to 97 US government computers during a separate investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/latest-on-gary-mckinnon-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Latest on Gary Mc Kinnon case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Latest on Gary McKinnon case - GOV.UK...</span></span></span>

The resemblance therefore lies mainly in broad themes—interest in hidden information and exploitation of weak computer security—not in evidence of coordinated action.

## How later retellings merged separate histories

Popular accounts often compress multiple cybercrime stories into a single narrative because doing so produces a cleaner and more dramatic history. Several factors encourage this blending.

First, all three hackers were British and all became associated with US military systems. Second, later media coverage gave McKinnon enormous public visibility through his decade-long extradition battle, prompting retrospective comparisons with earlier British hackers. Third, references to UFOs became the most memorable aspect of both Bevan's and McKinnon's stories, even though their investigations focused primarily on [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to government computers rather than confirming extraterrestrial evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmhansrd/cm090715/debtext/90715-0012.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Commons Hansard Debates for 15 July 2009 (pt 0012)July 15, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2009</span></span></span>

As these stories circulated online and in documentaries, distinctions in chronology gradually disappeared. Separate incidents became retold as though they represented successive stages of one continuing search for alien secrets.

## What the historical evidence actually supports

The strongest evidence supports a much narrower conclusion than many popular retellings suggest.

* Bevan and Pryce were connected through the same mid-1990s hacking environment.
* McKinnon carried out his better-known intrusions many years later.
* All three exploited weak computer security, but in different investigations.
* There is no credible documentary evidence that the three formed an organised team, shared operational planning or conducted a coordinated search for UFO information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmhansrd/cm090715/debtext/90715-0012.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Commons Hansard Debates for 15 July 2009 (pt 0012)July 15, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 15, 2009</span></span></span>

The label "Britain's UFO hackers" is therefore useful only as a broad descriptive category. It should not be understood as referring to a single coordinated group or collaborative operation.
