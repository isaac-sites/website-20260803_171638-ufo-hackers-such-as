## Introduction

Gary McKinnon consistently said that his search of United States military and NASA computers was motivated not simply by curiosity about UFOs, but by a belief that governments were concealing transformative propulsion and energy technologies. A major influence on that belief was the Disclosure Project, launched by physician Steven Greer and publicised through its May 2001 National Press Club event. Rather than convincing McKinnon that extraterrestrial technology had definitely been recovered, the project persuaded him that testimony from former military, intelligence and aerospace personnel was credible enough to justify looking for documentary evidence himself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

Understanding that distinction is important. The Disclosure Project shaped McKinnon's motivation and search strategy, but it did not independently verify the claims it promoted. McKinnon's own statements describe why he considered the witnesses persuasive; they do not establish that the alleged technologies existed or that his computer intrusions uncovered proof of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why the Disclosure Project became McKinnon's starting point

The Disclosure Project presented itself as more than a collection of UFO sighting reports. At its 9 May 2001 press conference in Washington, it assembled former military officers, intelligence personnel, aviation officials and government employees who claimed first-hand knowledge of secret UFO programmes. Alongside discussion of unidentified craft, organisers argued that governments had concealed advanced propulsion systems and revolutionary energy technologies allegedly derived from recovered vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2001/05/ooo-wee-ooo-fans-come-to-d-c/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ooo-WEE-ooo Fans Come to D.C. &#124; WIRED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ooo-WEE-ooo Fans Come to D.C. &#124; WIRED...</span></span></span>

For McKinnon, this framing changed the nature of the question. Instead of searching merely for evidence that UFOs existed, he became interested in whether official computer systems might contain records of engineering projects, classified spacecraft or propulsion research. He later described hacking as "a means to an end", with that end being verification of claims already made publicly by Disclosure Project witnesses rather than random exploration of [government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## What the Disclosure Project alleged

The specific allegations that influenced McKinnon extended beyond extraterrestrial visitation. The Disclosure Project argued that:

* recovered non-human craft had been studied in secret;
* some technologies had allegedly been reverse-engineered;
* antigravity propulsion systems existed within [classified programmes]({{ 'secret-projects/' | relative_url }});
* advanced energy systems capable of replacing conventional fuels had been deliberately withheld from public use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.disclosureinstitute.org/testimony/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosureinstitute.org">[disclosureinstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosureinstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Testimony – The Disclosure Institute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Testimony – The Disclosure Institute</span></span></span>

These claims mattered because they offered McKinnon concrete categories of information to search for. His later interviews repeatedly mention antigravity, "[free energy]({{ 'free-energy/' | relative_url }})" and suppressed technology alongside UFO evidence, reflecting the Disclosure Project's own emphasis rather than a separate line of inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why McKinnon considered its witnesses credible

McKinnon repeatedly explained that what impressed him was not the extraordinary nature of the claims but the backgrounds of the people making them.

In BBC and later interviews, he referred to the Disclosure Project's witnesses as "very credible, relied-upon people", describing them as including military radar operators, air traffic controllers, intelligence personnel and officials connected with nuclear command responsibilities. He argued that such individuals had occupied positions requiring trust and technical expertise, making their testimony worthy of investigation even if it remained unproven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.checktheevidence.com/wordpress/2006/05/06/old-news-1805/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: checktheevidence.com">[checktheevidence.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">checktheevidence.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Check The Evidence Hacker – Gary Mc Kinnon – BBC Interview – Check The Evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Check The Evidence Hacker – Gary Mc Kinnon – BBC Interview – Check The Evidence</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important feature of McKinnon's reasoning. He did not present himself as beginning with evidence recovered from government systems. Instead, he began with public witness testimony that he regarded as sufficiently authoritative to justify searching classified networks for corroborating documentation.

That distinction also explains why he focused on particular agencies. When Disclosure Project witness Donna Hare claimed that NASA personnel removed UFOs from satellite imagery, McKinnon treated the allegation as a specific investigative lead, directing attention towards NASA systems rather than searching indiscriminately across unrelated government databases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.checktheevidence.com/wordpress/2006/05/06/old-news-1805/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: checktheevidence.com">[Check The Evidence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">checktheevidence.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Check The Evidence Hacker – Gary Mc Kinnon – BBC Interview – Check The Evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Check The Evidence Hacker – Gary Mc Kinnon – BBC Interview – Check The Evidence</span></span></span>

## The gap between motivation and evidence

Although the Disclosure Project clearly shaped McKinnon's objectives, that influence should not be confused with validation of the underlying allegations.

McKinnon's interviews consistently describe what he believed he saw and why he searched for it, but the existence of sincere belief does not establish the existence of hidden antigravity or free-energy technologies. Likewise, the Disclosure Project's presentation relied primarily on witness testimony rather than publicly verifiable technical documentation demonstrating operational propulsion or energy systems. [disclosureinstitute.org+2WIRED](https://www.disclosureinstitute.org/testimony/)

As a result, historians of the case generally distinguish between two separate questions. One concerns McKinnon's motivation, which is well documented through his own interviews. The other concerns whether the technologies described by Disclosure Project witnesses actually existed, a claim that remains unverified by publicly available evidence despite its central role in shaping McKinnon's search.
