## Introduction

One of the most specific damage allegations in the prosecution of Gary McKinnon was that he deleted **2,455 user accounts** from a United States Army computer responsible for controlling access to an Army network. According to US authorities, this was not simply the removal of individual usernames: they alleged that deleting the accounts from a central administrative system caused connected computers to reboot and become unusable until administrators restored the affected directory information. McKinnon admitted accessing US military computers without authorisation but consistently denied intentionally causing damage. Because his extradition case never proceeded to a criminal trial in the United States, the allegation was never tested through cross-examination or a verdict on the evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

## How the account-control computer worked

The allegation centred on a **single administrative computer**, not on thousands of separate machines being individually compromised.

Modern military and enterprise Windows networks commonly rely on central directory or account-management services. Rather than storing every user's credentials locally on each workstation, a central server maintains user accounts, permissions and authentication data. Workstations consult that central service whenever users log in or when the operating system validates access to network resources.

In practical terms, this architecture means that compromising one privileged administrative system can have consequences far beyond the machine initially accessed. If administrative accounts or directory records are deleted from the central management system, users may be unable to authenticate, security policies can fail, and dependent computers may repeatedly attempt to reconnect or reboot while waiting for valid account information. Restoring service generally requires rebuilding or recovering the directory database rather than simply restarting individual computers.

The House of Lords' summary of the prosecution case described the affected machine as **"a US Army computer that controlled access to an Army computer network"**, reflecting this centralised role rather than suggesting that thousands of separate computers were independently hacked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

3. Computers dependent on those accounts began rebooting and became inoperable.

This allegation differs from another Army-related accusation in the McKinnon case involving deletion of operating system files from computers supporting the [Military District]({{ 'network-shutdown/' | relative_url }}) of Washington network. The account-deletion allegation concerns **identity and access management**, whereas the operating-system allegation concerns removal of critical system files. Although both appeared within the same prosecution narrative, they describe different technical mechanisms for disrupting network operations. [UK Parliament](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm)

From a systems administration perspective, deleting centrally managed user accounts can produce widespread disruption without physically damaging hardware. Networked computers may fail authentication checks, lose access to shared resources or repeatedly restart while attempting to apply security policies. Such effects arise because many machines depend on the same directory service rather than maintaining independent copies of user information.

## Why attribution remained contested

The existence of the allegation itself is well documented. Its factual accuracy, however, remained legally unresolved.

The House of Lords accepted the American allegations as sufficient for extradition proceedings but emphasised that extradition hearings are **not criminal trials** determining guilt. Their purpose was to decide whether the legal requirements for extradition were met, not whether every alleged act of damage had been proved beyond reasonable doubt. [UK Parliament](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm)

McKinnon acknowledged [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to military and NASA computers but denied deliberately destroying systems. In interviews and public statements he portrayed himself as searching for information relating to UFOs, advanced technology and government secrecy rather than attempting to sabotage military operations. His position was that he had not intentionally caused the extensive operational damage described by prosecutors. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/latest-on-gary-mckinnon-case)

Because extradition was ultimately halted by the UK government on [human-rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}) grounds, no US criminal trial occurred. As a result:

* the prosecution never presented its forensic evidence before a jury;
* defence experts never cross-examined the government's technical witnesses;
* no judicial findings determined whether McKinnon personally deleted the 2,455 accounts or whether the resulting outages occurred exactly as alleged.

The allegation therefore remains an important element of the prosecution's case rather than a fact established through a completed criminal conviction. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/latest-on-gary-mckinnon-case)

## Why this allegation mattered

Among the various damage allegations, the claimed deletion of **2,455 Army user accounts** illustrated an important point about network administration: a single privileged administrative system can become a critical point of failure.

The prosecution relied on this allegation to argue that the consequences of the intrusion extended beyond viewing sensitive information. Instead, it presented the incident as an example of how deleting centrally managed authentication data could disable large numbers of dependent computers and require substantial recovery work. That distinction helped support the broader claim that the case involved operational damage to defence networks rather than merely unauthorised access. [Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm)
