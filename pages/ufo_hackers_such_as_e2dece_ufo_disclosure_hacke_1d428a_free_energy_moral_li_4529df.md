## Introduction

Gary McKinnon consistently argued that his [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to US military and NASA computer systems was driven by more than curiosity about UFOs. He said he believed governments were concealing revolutionary propulsion and energy technologies—particularly antigravity systems and "free energy" or "[zero-point energy]({{ 'zero-point-energy/' | relative_url }})"—that could transform society if released. In his own account, this belief changed the moral meaning of his actions. Rather than viewing himself as stealing information or trespassing on protected networks, he portrayed his intrusions as an attempt to recover knowledge that had been unjustly withheld from the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

That explanation is important because it illustrates a broader mechanism within UFO [disclosure culture]({{ 'disclosure-culture/' | relative_url }}). Claims that hidden technologies could eliminate energy shortages, reduce poverty and reshape civilisation gave some believers a powerful ethical rationale for seeking classified information. Those beliefs do not establish that such technologies existed, but they help explain how McKinnon came to see unlawful access as serving a higher public purpose.

## The alleged technologies McKinnon hoped to find

McKinnon's public statements repeatedly linked UFO secrecy with advanced energy research rather than extraterrestrial life alone. In interviews, he said he believed governments had suppressed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Antigravity propulsion.
* "Free energy" technologies.
* Zero-point energy systems capable of producing abundant power.
* Reverse-engineered technology allegedly derived from recovered non-human craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

</div>

His views were heavily influenced by the [Disclosure Project]({{ 'disclosure-influence/' | relative_url }}), launched by physician Steven Greer, whose 2001 Washington press conference and accompanying witness testimonies argued that governments possessed hidden knowledge about UFOs and advanced technologies. McKinnon later said those presentations convinced him that credible insiders had already revealed enough evidence to justify looking for documentary confirmation inside government systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_McKinnon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary Mc Kinnon</span></span></span>

Crucially, McKinnon was not merely searching for confirmation that UFOs existed. He claimed he expected to find practical technologies whose suppression, if true, would have direct consequences for ordinary people.

## How public benefit claims lowered the moral barrier

The most revealing aspect of McKinnon's explanation is not the specific technology he believed in but the ethical reasoning built around it.

In a widely quoted interview with *WIRED*, he explained his motivation by saying he believed governments were suppressing "antigravity, UFO-related technologies, free energy or what they call zero-point energy" and added that such knowledge "should not be kept hidden from the public when pensioners can't pay their fuel bills."<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

That statement reframed the issue in moral rather than technical terms.

Instead of asking:

* *Is it legal to enter these systems?*

the reasoning became:

* *Is it acceptable to leave humanity deprived of transformative technology?*

Within that framework, computer intrusion became a form of whistleblowing rather than ordinary cybercrime. If officials were hiding technology capable of eliminating energy scarcity, then exposing it could be portrayed as serving a greater [public interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}).

This reasoning resembles what ethicists sometimes describe as "higher-law" justification: the belief that violating one rule is permissible because doing so prevents a much greater wrong. McKinnon repeatedly described hacking as merely "a means to an end", suggesting that the objective—not the intrusion itself—was morally decisive in his own thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

## Why free energy beliefs carried unusual emotional force

Many conspiracy theories focus on hidden historical events or secret political decisions. Claims about suppressed free-energy technology are different because they promise immediate improvements to everyday life.

If such technology genuinely existed, believers argue it could:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* reduce dependence on fossil fuels;
* eliminate fuel poverty;
* transform transportation through antigravity propulsion;
* reshape global economics.

</div>

Because the alleged benefits are humanitarian rather than merely scientific, believers may regard disclosure as a moral obligation rather than an intellectual curiosity.

McKinnon's own comments about pensioners struggling with heating costs illustrate this shift. The argument was no longer simply that governments knew something extraordinary. It was that ordinary people were allegedly suffering because officials concealed technology that could alleviate hardship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

That perceived injustice helped lower the psychological barrier to unauthorised access. If the hidden information was imagined as belonging to humanity rather than to the state, then retrieving it could appear ethically justified despite being legally prohibited.

## Why good intentions did not make the access lawful

McKinnon's stated motive became an important feature of public discussion surrounding his case, particularly during the long extradition proceedings. However, motive and legality are distinct questions.

US prosecutors alleged that he unlawfully accessed numerous military and NASA computer systems and caused significant disruption. The criminal allegations were based on unauthorised access and damage to protected computer networks, not on whether his beliefs about UFOs or energy technology were sincere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/04/terrorist-or-ufo-truth-seeker/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">terrorist or ufo truth seeker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Terrorist or UFO Truth Seeker? &#124; WIREDApril 28, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 28, 2006</span></span></span>

## Separating motive from evidence

An important distinction remains between McKinnon's motivation and the evidence for the technologies he hoped to uncover.

McKinnon consistently maintained that he found material supporting his suspicions, including claims about unusual spacecraft imagery and documents referring to "non-terrestrial officers". Those claims have never been independently verified through publicly available documentary evidence, and government agencies have not confirmed that the alleged discoveries demonstrate secret extraterrestrial or advanced energy programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WIRED&#x27;UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

For understanding his actions, however, verification is not the central issue. The crucial mechanism is that belief in suppressed clean-energy technology transformed unauthorised computer access into what McKinnon regarded as a public-interest mission. Within the broader culture of UFO disclosure, the promise of antigravity and free energy supplied not only something worth searching for, but also an ethical narrative that made crossing legal boundaries appear, in his own view, morally defensible—even though the law treated the intrusions as criminal acts.
