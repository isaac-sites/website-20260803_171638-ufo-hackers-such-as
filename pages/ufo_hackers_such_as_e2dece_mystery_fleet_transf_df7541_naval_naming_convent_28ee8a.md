## Introduction

One of the assumptions behind Gary McKinnon's recollection of unfamiliar ship names is that a genuine United States Navy vessel should be easy to identify through a straightforward name search. In practice, US naval administration is considerably more complicated. Commissioned warships, civilian-crewed support vessels, service craft, leased ships, renamed vessels and hull classification systems all use different naming conventions. As a result, an unfamiliar name—or the inability to find one quickly—does not by itself indicate the existence of a secret fleet.

This matters because McKinnon did not preserve the spreadsheet he described, nor did he record the ship names he believed were unusual. Any attempt to verify his recollection retrospectively therefore depends on understanding how the Navy actually identifies vessels and how those systems can frustrate simple searches. Rather than proving or disproving his claim, naval naming conventions illustrate why retrospective verification is more difficult than it first appears.

## The Ship Naming Rules McKinnon Could Have Missed

### USS, USNS and vessels merely in service

Many casual searches assume every naval vessel carries the prefix **USS** ("United States Ship"). In reality, that designation applies only to commissioned Navy vessels. Ships operated by the Military Sealift Command are generally designated **USNS** ("United States Naval Ship"), because they are civilian-crewed vessels in active service rather than commissioned warships. Other Navy service craft may have no USS or USNS prefix at all and can instead be identified primarily by their hull classification and number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/heritage/customs-and-traditions0/ship-naming.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command Ship Naming</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandShip NamingMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span>

This distinction creates several opportunities for confusion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A search limited to "USS" names may miss active naval vessels operating under the **USNS** prefix.
* Smaller support craft may never have carried a familiar prefix.
* Administrative documents may refer to vessels by hull designation rather than by a recognisable public name.
* Logistics systems may abbreviate vessel identities differently from public historical sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/heritage/customs-and-traditions0/ship-naming.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command Ship Naming</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandShip NamingMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span>

</div>

Consequently, someone encountering an unfamiliar spreadsheet entry without surrounding context could reasonably fail to locate an immediate public match, even if the vessel itself were entirely conventional.

### Hull classifications, renamed ships and unnamed craft

The Navy also identifies vessels through **hull classification symbols**, combinations of letters and numbers describing the vessel's function rather than simply its name. Examples include destroyers (DDG), aircraft carriers (CVN), amphibious assault ships (LHA) and numerous auxiliary categories. Federal law authorises the Navy to establish and modify these classifications as operational needs change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section8661" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

Names themselves are not fixed identifiers over a vessel's entire life.

Several factors complicate historical searches:

* **Renaming.** The Secretary of the Navy has statutory authority to rename certain vessels, particularly acquired ships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section8662" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 25, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Changing naming traditions.** Different classes have followed different naming conventions over time. Attack submarines, cruisers and other ship types have all shifted between themes such as states, cities, battles and individuals across different eras.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/content/dam/nhhc/research/library/online-reading-room/ships/images/navy-ship-names-crs-report-sept-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command Navy Ship Names: Background for Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandNavy Ship Names: Background for CongressJune 13, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 13, 2025</span></span></span>
* **Auxiliary and support vessels.** Many logistics ships attract far less public attention than combat vessels, making them harder to recognise from memory alone.
* **Service craft.** Some smaller craft are identified primarily by classification and hull number rather than by a distinctive ship name. Navy regulations explicitly distinguish these from commissioned vessels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/heritage/customs-and-traditions0/ship-naming.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.navy.mil">[Naval History and Heritage Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Naval History and Heritage Command Ship Naming</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Naval History and Heritage CommandShip NamingMay 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2026</span></span></span>

These administrative practices mean that "I could not find the ship name" is not equivalent to "the ship did not officially exist."

## How naming conventions affect retrospective verification

McKinnon's account presents a particularly difficult verification problem because none of the crucial identifying information survives. He has consistently said he cannot remember the ship names, only that they appeared unfamiliar and did not correspond to vessels he could identify publicly.

Even if investigators wished to test the claim today, they would face several obstacles created by naval naming practice:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Without the precise spelling, even minor differences in wording could point to completely different vessels.
* A remembered name might have belonged to a support ship rather than a commissioned combatant.
* Administrative spreadsheets could have used abbreviations, hull numbers or shortened forms rather than formal public names.
* A vessel might later have been renamed, transferred or removed from active service.

</div>

The official Naval Vessel Register is itself not a complete historical catalogue. It records ships and service craft through their operational life cycle but acknowledges that vessels disposed of before 1987 are still being added gradually to the historical database. Therefore, absence from a modern search does not automatically demonstrate that a historical vessel never existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Resources/Naval-Vessel-Register/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navsea.navy.mil">[NAVSEA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navsea.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NAVSEANaval Sea Systems Command &gt; Resources &gt; Naval Vessel Register</span></span></span>

For these reasons, naval nomenclature weakens one of the central inferences sometimes drawn from McKinnon's recollections. An unsuccessful search for an unfamiliar vessel name cannot, on its own, distinguish between several possibilities: an incorrectly remembered name, an obscure auxiliary vessel, an administrative shorthand, a renamed ship, an incomplete historical record or, less likely, a genuinely [classified programme]({{ 'secret-projects/' | relative_url }}). Because the underlying spreadsheet was never preserved, the naming conventions themselves become part of the reason the claim remains impossible to verify conclusively.
