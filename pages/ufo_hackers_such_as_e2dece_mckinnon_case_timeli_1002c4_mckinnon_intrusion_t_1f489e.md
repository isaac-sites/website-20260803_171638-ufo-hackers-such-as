## Introduction

The differing totals reported in US records about Gary McKinnon's computer intrusions—most commonly **97**, **92**, and **six** computers—are not contradictory descriptions of the same evidence. Instead, they reflect how different legal documents counted different groups of alleged victims for different purposes. The broader extradition record summarised all of the US government computers allegedly accessed during the investigation, while the Virginia indictment focused only on the systems within that court's [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}) and separately counted a small group of private-sector computers. Understanding these distinctions helps explain why multiple figures appear throughout [official records]({{ 'official-record/' | relative_url }}) without indicating that investigators changed their core allegations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

## The 97 government-computer figure

The figure of **97 US government computers** became the best-known overall total because it was used in the formal extradition proceedings between the United States and the United Kingdom.

The House of Lords judgment summarising the extradition request states that the United States alleged McKinnon gained [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to **97 US government computers** between **1 February 2001 and 19 [March 2002]({{ 'march-2002/' | relative_url }})**. The judgment also breaks those systems down by agency:

* **53 Army computers**
* **26 Navy computers**
* **16 NASA computers**
* **1 Department of Defense computer**
* **1 Air Force computer**

</div>

These categories total **97 government-owned systems**. The judgment presents this as the overall scope of the alleged government intrusions rather than as the contents of a single criminal indictment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>

This broader figure therefore serves as a case-wide summary, bringing together the alleged conduct across multiple military branches and civilian agencies during the investigation.

## What the Virginia indictment counted

The **Eastern District of Virginia** indictment used a different counting method because it charged only the conduct falling within that federal court's jurisdiction.

According to the Department of Justice announcement, the Virginia grand jury alleged that between **March 2001 and March 2002** McKinnon:

* accessed **92 computers** belonging to the US Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA; and
* accessed **six computers belonging to private businesses**.

The indictment therefore distinguishes between **government systems** and **private-sector systems** instead of presenting one combined total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

Importantly, the Virginia indictment does **not** claim responsibility for every alleged intrusion investigated by US authorities. It was a charging document designed around the offences prosecutable in that district rather than a comprehensive inventory of every affected computer.

## How jurisdiction shaped the published totals

The apparent inconsistency largely disappears once the different jurisdictions are considered.

In **November 2002**, US prosecutors announced **two separate indictments**:

* a **Virginia** indictment covering the broader pattern of alleged military, NASA and related intrusions within that district's jurisdiction; and
* a **New Jersey** indictment centred on alleged activity involving the Naval Weapons Station Earle network.

The New Jersey press release explicitly noted that the two federal districts would conduct **separate prosecutions** and described the Virginia indictment as covering the **92 military and NASA systems** plus the **six private-company computers**, while the New Jersey case addressed the separate allegations concerning Naval Weapons Station Earle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Naval Weapons Station Computers, Disabling Network After Sept. 11 (N...</span></span></span>

Because federal criminal jurisdiction in the United States is organised by judicial districts, prosecutors often divide complex cyber cases according to where particular offences occurred or where affected systems were located. The resulting indictments therefore need not reproduce identical victim counts.

## Why the numbers are not a factual contradiction

## The key takeaway

The varying totals in US records stem from **legal structure rather than evidential inconsistency**. The extradition documents summarised the full allegation of access to **97 government computers**, whereas the Virginia indictment charged **92 government systems** within its jurisdiction and separately identified **six private-company computers**. Reading the documents in their procedural context shows that the differing numbers reflect the way US federal prosecutions divided the alleged conduct, not a disagreement over the existence of the underlying investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and AnotherJuly 30, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 30, 2008</span></span></span>
