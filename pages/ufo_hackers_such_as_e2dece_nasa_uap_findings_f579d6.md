## Introduction

NASA’s 2023 study of unidentified anomalous phenomena, or UAP, did not find credible evidence that UFO reports represent extraterrestrial spacecraft or alien technology. Its more important conclusion was methodological: most reported incidents do not contain enough reliable information to determine confidently what was observed. Images may lack original files and processing histories; sensors may be uncalibrated; distance, weather and viewing geometry may be unknown; and a sighting may have no independent measurement against which it can be checked. NASA therefore concluded that the present evidence base is too incomplete for firm scientific claims, whether ordinary or extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

That finding provides a useful standard for assessing older stories about UFO-motivated hackers such as Gary McKinnon. Access to a government computer, even when admitted or independently established, does not authenticate every interpretation of what was allegedly seen inside it. NASA’s framework asks a different set of questions: Is the original material preserved? Is its source known? Are the instruments, metadata and processing steps documented? Can independent investigators reproduce the analysis? Without those safeguards, a dramatic recollection remains a claim rather than scientific evidence.

## What NASA’s UAP study actually concluded

NASA commissioned a 16-member independent team to consider how the agency could contribute to the scientific study of UAP. The assignment concentrated on available data, future collection methods and analytical tools. It was not an investigation of every famous UFO case, nor an audit of alleged secret programmes or historical claims of recovered alien technology. The team used unclassified information and published its final report in September 2023.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

The report reached three closely related conclusions.

**No conclusive extraterrestrial evidence was identified.** NASA stated that the peer-reviewed scientific literature contained no conclusive evidence that UAP had an extraterrestrial origin. Its current public guidance is similarly direct: the agency says it has found no credible evidence of extraterrestrial life and no evidence that UAP are alien technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

**Some observations remain unidentified, but “unidentified” is not a physical explanation.** A case can remain unresolved because the necessary measurements were never made, not because the object displayed technology beyond human capabilities. NASA treated “UAP” as a description of an observation that has not yet been satisfactorily identified, rather than as a category of craft or a synonym for extraterrestrial visitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

**The main obstacle is the weakness of the dataset.** NASA found that the limited number of high-quality observations made it impossible to draw firm scientific conclusions about UAP as a class. The report therefore functioned primarily as a roadmap for gathering better evidence in the future, rather than as a final explanation of past incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. NASA did not claim to have proved that extraterrestrial visitors could never exist. It found that the material available to its study did not justify concluding that alien technology had been observed. That is an absence of adequate supporting evidence, not a philosophical declaration that the hypothesis is impossible.

## Why poor data keeps objects unidentified

A photograph or video can look compelling while still being scientifically weak. To determine an object’s size, speed and nature, an analyst may need the original recording, precise time and location, sensor specifications, camera angle, focal length, platform movement, range information, environmental conditions and details of any compression or enhancement. When several of those elements are missing, very different physical explanations can produce similar-looking imagery.

NASA highlighted four recurring deficiencies: poor [sensor calibration]({{ 'sensor-calibration/' | relative_url }}), a lack of multiple measurements, missing sensor metadata and inadequate baseline data. Each one limits what can safely be inferred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

**Calibration** establishes how accurately an instrument measures brightness, angle, distance, temperature or motion. Without it, an apparent feature may reflect the behaviour of the equipment rather than the object.

**Multiple measurements** allow one sensor’s interpretation to be checked against another. A visual camera, infrared system and radar recording the same event can jointly constrain its position and movement far better than a single clip.

**Metadata** supplies the context surrounding a recording: when and where it was made, which sensor mode was used, how the platform was moving and whether the file was modified. Stripping that information from an image can remove much of its evidential value.

**Baseline data** describes what normally appears in the same environment. Investigators need reliable information about aircraft, balloons, drones, satellites, atmospheric effects, sensor artefacts and other ordinary phenomena before deciding that an observation is genuinely anomalous. NASA noted that anomaly detection depends on understanding the background population: it is difficult to identify the unusual without first modelling the normal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

Eyewitness testimony can still help identify patterns, locations or times of interest. NASA nevertheless stressed that testimony alone is not reproducible and normally lacks the measurements required to determine an object’s origin. A credible witness may accurately report an unusual experience while being unable to establish distance, scale or velocity. Credibility of the observer and completeness of the evidence are separate questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## The “GoFast” example shows how geometry changes a claim

NASA’s discussion of the US Navy “GoFast” video illustrates why raw appearance is not enough. The infrared footage gives the impression that a small object is travelling at great speed just above the sea. By using information displayed by the aircraft’s sensor and accounting for the aircraft’s own movement, the study team produced a less extraordinary interpretation.

The report estimated that the object could have been roughly 13,000 feet above the ocean rather than close to the surface. It calculated an average movement of about 40 miles per hour during the analysed interval—a speed compatible with drifting in the wind at that altitude. The powerful impression of rapid motion was largely attributable to parallax, in which a nearby observer or moving camera makes a more distant object appear to sweep rapidly across the background. The report acknowledged uncertainty, but found no need to invoke extraordinary propulsion to explain the visible motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

This example does not prove that every UAP video has the same explanation. It demonstrates a broader evidential lesson: apparent performance cannot be treated as measured performance. Claims that an object accelerated impossibly, crossed vast distances or defied aerodynamics require dependable range, timing and platform data. A striking image without those measurements may support several incompatible interpretations.

It also shows why “unresolved” should not be confused with “unexplainable”. Additional telemetry might identify an object conclusively, but if that information was never captured or preserved, the case may remain open indefinitely. The unresolved label then describes the limits of the surviving record, not necessarily the properties of the object.

## What better UAP evidence would look like

NASA recommended moving away from accidental, isolated recordings towards systematic observation. The strongest future cases would combine several well-calibrated sensors, preserve complete metadata and collect measurements in forms that independent teams could examine. NASA’s Earth-observing archives may help reconstruct weather and environmental conditions around reported events, while commercial satellites could occasionally provide higher-resolution coincident imagery. The report cautioned, however, that many NASA satellites were designed to study broad features of Earth rather than small airborne objects, so their usefulness depends on resolution, coverage and timing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

The team also supported more standardised reporting by pilots and the public. Stigma can reduce the number of reports and discourage trained observers from supplying timely information. NASA argued that treating UAP as an ordinary scientific and aviation-safety question could improve participation and produce a broader dataset. [Better reporting]({{ 'better-reporting/' | relative_url }}) alone would not prove an exotic explanation, but it could ensure that valuable contextual details are recorded before they disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/8b477a5ed6a42f99bb13a4518368ce9a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The findings from a yearlong study suggest overcoming the stigma attached to UFO research is essential for data collection. Artificial intelligence and machine learning may eventually help search large datasets for rare or unusual events, but NASA rejected the idea that algorithms can repair fundamentally deficient evidence. A model trained on poorly characterised observations may amplify errors, mistake common objects for anomalies or simply reproduce the biases in its input. The report said these techniques become useful only after researchers have built extensive, well-curated datasets describing both known phenomena and genuine outliers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, a persuasive claim of alien technology would require much more than a puzzling image. Investigators would look for preserved original data, documented custody, independent sensor confirmation, calculable location and movement, excluded conventional explanations, transparent analysis and results that other specialists could test. The more extraordinary the conclusion, the more important each link in that chain becomes.

## How NASA’s standard applies to hacker claims

UFO hacking stories create an unusual evidential problem because two claims are often blended together. The first is that a person obtained [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) to a computer system. That may be supported by [court records]({{ 'court-record/' | relative_url }}), admissions, network logs or forensic findings. The second is that material encountered during that access proved the existence of alien spacecraft or secret extraterrestrial programmes. The first claim does not establish the second.

Under NASA’s evidence standard, an alleged image glimpsed remotely would need the original file, its server location, creation and modification history, instrument provenance, accompanying metadata and evidence that the image had not been mislabelled, processed for an ordinary operational purpose or misunderstood. A filename or heading would also need administrative context. Terms that sound extraordinary in isolation may have conventional military, organisational or technical meanings.

The weakness is especially severe when nothing was saved. A remembered screen image cannot be re-examined for compression artefacts, processing traces, scale, sensor mode or environmental context. An alleged list cannot be checked for authorship, date, surrounding records or the meaning assigned to its terminology. Even sincere testimony cannot replace the missing digital object and its provenance.

NASA’s analysis therefore neither verifies nor specifically disproves Gary McKinnon’s reported recollections. The 2023 team did not conduct a forensic review of his intrusions or search the systems he accessed two decades earlier. What it supplies is a formal measure of evidential sufficiency. By that measure, unpreserved observations and unsupported interpretations fall far below what would be needed to establish alien technology. NASA itself emphasised that its report was forward-looking and was not an assessment of individual historical incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## The central distinction: mystery is not confirmation

NASA’s position is more careful than either blanket dismissal or extraterrestrial advocacy. It accepts that people and sensors sometimes record things that remain unidentified. It also insists that an unexplained residue cannot automatically be assigned the most dramatic available cause.

Three statements can therefore be true at the same time:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A witness may have encountered something genuinely puzzling.
* The surviving information may be insufficient to identify it.
* There may still be no positive evidence that it was alien technology.

</div>

This is the most useful connection between NASA’s UAP review and older UFO hacking narratives. Computer access may reveal authentic files, but authenticity, interpretation and extraordinary origin must each be established separately. Where original data, metadata and independent confirmation are missing, the honest conclusion is not that extraterrestrial technology has been discovered. It is that the claim cannot be reliably tested.

NASA’s study shifts the emphasis from who tells the most compelling story to which observation can survive measurement, verification and attempted refutation. For claims associated with UFO hackers, that standard leaves the central alien-technology allegations unsubstantiated—not because secrecy is impossible, but because the evidence needed to demonstrate them has not been produced.

      });

        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
