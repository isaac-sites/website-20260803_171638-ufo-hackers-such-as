## Introduction

When discussions of UFO-related hacking, including the claims made by Gary McKinnon, focus on a single document, spreadsheet or image, an important question is often overlooked: what information about that file has been lost? A digital document rarely stands on its visible text alone. Its authorship, creation date, modification history, folder location, linked records and system metadata all help establish what the file actually represents. Without that context, even a genuine government file can be misunderstood.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_government_file_misi_12aa80_missing_metadata_fil_6d844c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Missing Metadata illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_government_file_misi_12aa80_missing_metadata_fil_6d844c-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_government_file_misi_12aa80_missing_metadata_fil_6d844c-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This matters because government computer systems are working environments rather than curated archives. Records management standards treat metadata—the descriptive and technical information surrounding a file—as essential evidence of provenance. Once that evidence disappears, it becomes much harder to determine whether a document was a final decision, an abandoned draft, an external attachment, a copied reference, a training exercise or something else entirely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/policy/metadata-compiled" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesMetadata Requirements for Permanent Electronic Records &#124; National ArchivesApril 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2025</span></span></span>

## What metadata establishes a file's provenance?

Metadata is often described simply as "data about data", but for investigators it serves a more practical role: it explains where a file came from and how it reached its present form.

For government records, particularly electronic records intended for long-term preservation, archivists commonly seek information including:

NIST defines provenance as the chronology of a system or data's origin, ownership, location and changes over time. In other words, provenance records the history needed to explain why a digital object exists in its current state rather than merely proving that it exists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/provenance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.gov">[NIST Computer Security Resource Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Computer Security Resource Centerprovenance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIST Computer Security Resource Centerprovenance - Glossary &#124; CSRC...</span></span></span>

## How folder structure and document history shape meaning

A file's location within an organisation frequently conveys information that disappears once the file is copied elsewhere.

For example, the same document could have very different meanings depending on whether it originally resided in:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a user's temporary working folder
* a contractor submission directory
* an intelligence analysis workspace
* a legal review folder
* a software testing environment
* an archive of incoming correspondence.

</div>

Removing only the folder path eliminates evidence about the file's role in organisational work.

The UK National Archives explicitly preserves parent folder names for born-digital records because otherwise many filenames become almost meaningless. Recording the original directory structure allows future researchers to reconstruct relationships between documents and understand how records were organised when actively used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/professional-guidance-and-services/cataloguing-practices/digital-cataloguing-practices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Our digital cataloguing practices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesOur digital cataloguing practices - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Document history provides similar context. A visible sentence in a Word document may have been inserted yesterday, deleted the following week, or heavily qualified in later revisions. If only one extracted version survives, readers cannot determine whether they are seeing an early proposal, a heavily edited compromise or the final approved wording.

## Why screenshots and copied fragments weaken verification

One recurring difficulty in disputed government-document claims is that the public often sees only screenshots, excerpts or recollections rather than the original digital record.

A screenshot usually preserves only visual appearance. It often omits:

* embedded metadata

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* document properties
* creation timestamps
* revision history
* digital signatures
* hyperlinks
* hidden comments
* associated attachments
* surrounding directory structure.

</div>

Likewise, copied text pasted into another document loses much of the contextual information needed to authenticate and interpret it.

Even where screenshots are genuine, they answer only limited questions:

* Was these words displayed?
* Did the screen resemble an official system?

They generally cannot answer equally important questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who authored the document?
* Was it ever approved?
* Was it later corrected?
* Was it received from another organisation?
* What documents surrounded it?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/archival-control-model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prov.vic.gov.au">[prov.vic.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prov.vic.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archival control model</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archival control model</span></span></span>
* Did other records contradict it?

</div>

Government guidance on metadata consistently treats this contextual information as necessary for future interpretation and validation rather than optional administrative detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/record-information-about-data-sets-you-share-with-others" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Record information about data sets you share with others</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Record information about data sets you share with others</span></span></span>

## Why this matters when assessing McKinnon's reported discoveries

Gary McKinnon has consistently described observing material including high-resolution imagery and a spreadsheet referring to "non-terrestrial officers" while accessing NASA and US military computers. Because he did not retain complete copies together with their associated metadata, independent researchers cannot reconstruct the original context of the material he reported seeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/scheduling/basics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Records Basics &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Basics &#124; National ArchivesJuly 28, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2025</span></span></span>

That absence of context leaves multiple plausible interpretations open.

The spreadsheet, if accurately remembered, might theoretically have been:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an operational document;
* an imported database from another source;
* a draft;
* a test dataset;
* an administrative list using terminology meaningful only within a local workflow; or
* something else entirely.

</div>

Without authorship, document history, neighbouring files and provenance metadata, there is no reliable way to distinguish among those possibilities.

Similarly, an image viewed on a government workstation cannot be fully interpreted from recollection alone. Investigators would ideally want to know its original filename, storage location, acquisition date, associated project, related documentation, embedded metadata and whether subsequent versions existed. Those contextual records frequently carry as much evidential value as the visible image itself.

This limitation does not prove that McKinnon's recollections were wrong. Nor does it validate them. Instead, it explains why the available evidence cannot easily establish the meaning of the reported files with confidence.

## Authenticity is only the starting point

A common misunderstanding is to equate authenticity with interpretation.

Several separate questions must be answered before a government file can support a significant claim:

QuestionWhy it mattersIs the file genuine?Confirms it existed.Who created it?Establishes responsibility and authority.When was it created and revised?Places it within organisational events.Where was it stored?Reveals organisational context.How did it relate to other records?Shows whether it supported, contradicted or supplemented other material.Was it final or preliminary?Determines institutional weight.

Metadata provides much of the evidence needed to answer these questions. Once that metadata is stripped away, investigators are left interpreting isolated words rather than documented organisational activity.

For this reason, archivists, digital forensic specialists and records managers consistently emphasise provenance and contextual metadata as essential components of a trustworthy electronic record. A document without its surrounding history may still be genuine, but its meaning can change dramatically once the information explaining its origin, relationships and evolution has vanished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/provenance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.gov">[nist.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Computer Security Resource Centerprovenance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIST Computer Security Resource Centerprovenance - Glossary &#124; CSRC...</span></span></span>
