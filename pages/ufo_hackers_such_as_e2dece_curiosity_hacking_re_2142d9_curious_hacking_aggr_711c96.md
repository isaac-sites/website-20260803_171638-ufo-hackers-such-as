## Introduction

A claim of curiosity does not, by itself, determine how seriously a hacking incident is treated. Courts and prosecutors generally look at what the intruder actually did: how long they remained inside a system, how many systems they accessed, whether they targeted particularly sensitive networks, whether they copied or altered data, and whether they tried to hide their actions. These factors help distinguish a brief episode of unauthorised exploration from conduct that creates substantial security risks or operational harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/computer-misuse-act" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Computer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceComputer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceAugust 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 2023</span></span></span>

## Duration, repetition and depth of access

One of the clearest aggravating factors is persistence. An isolated instance of unauthorised access may still constitute a criminal offence, but a campaign lasting weeks or months demonstrates repeated decisions to continue intruding after recognising that access is unauthorised.

Duration also tends to increase the opportunity for harm. A person who repeatedly returns to compromised systems can explore additional networks, obtain higher privileges, encounter more sensitive information and expose more victims. Courts therefore often view repeated access as evidence of greater culpability than a single exploratory incident.

Depth of access matters as much as duration. Simply reaching an exposed system differs from:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* obtaining administrator credentials;
* moving between internal networks;
* installing remote-access software;
* creating mechanisms for future access; or
* acquiring privileged accounts that allow broad control over a system.

</div>

In the allegations described during the McKinnon litigation, investigators claimed he installed remote administration software, scanned tens of thousands of government computers for additional vulnerable systems and moved between multiple military and [government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}) over more than a year. Those alleged actions illustrate why a curiosity-based explanation becomes less persuasive as the intrusion becomes increasingly systematic and technically sophisticated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

## Why military and government targets raise the stakes

Not every computer system carries the same potential consequences if compromised. Military, intelligence, defence and other government networks often support activities whose disruption could affect public safety, national security or essential government operations.

For that reason, prosecutors and courts commonly regard the nature of the target as an important consideration alongside the hacker's personal motive. The concern is not merely whether classified material was actually obtained, but whether the intrusion exposed systems whose compromise could have wider consequences than those affecting a private individual or commercial website.

The allegations in McKinnon's case involved access to US Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA computers. According to the House of Lords' summary of the indictment, the affected systems included networks associated with military administration, naval logistics and defence operations. Whether or not every allegation was ultimately proved, the choice of targets itself substantially increased the seriousness with which authorities viewed the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

This principle extends beyond military systems. Intrusions involving healthcare, emergency services, critical infrastructure or government administration are frequently treated more seriously because failures or interruptions may affect large numbers of people rather than only the system owner. The increased concern reflects the potential consequences of exposing sensitive systems, even where financial gain is absent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/computer-misuse-act" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Computer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceComputer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceAugust 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 2023</span></span></span>

## How copying, concealment and disruption increase culpability

Curiosity is least convincing when an intruder goes beyond simply viewing information.

Several actions substantially increase legal and practical concern:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Copying data.** Downloading files, credentials or databases creates continuing risks because information can be retained, shared or misused later.
* **Concealing activity.** Deleting logs, disguising software or masking identity suggests awareness of wrongdoing and can make forensic investigation more difficult.
* **Installing software.** Adding remote-access tools or persistence mechanisms transforms a temporary intrusion into an ongoing compromise.
* **Altering or deleting information.** Changes to operating systems, user accounts or files can impair reliability and disrupt legitimate users.
* **Interrupting services.** Operational disruption may affect organisations far beyond the immediate computer system.

</div>

The House of Lords judgment summarising the US allegations against McKinnon stated that investigators alleged he copied files and passwords from government systems, installed software intended to facilitate continued access and concealment, and deleted operating-system files and logs that allegedly rendered military networks temporarily inoperable. McKinnon admitted unauthorised access but disputed causing the alleged damage. The distinction is important: even where the extent of damage is contested, allegations of copying, concealment and disruption significantly influence how prosecutors and courts assess seriousness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

## Why curiosity becomes a weaker defence as harm increases

Courts generally distinguish between **motive** and **conduct**. A genuine fascination with UFOs, advanced technology or government secrecy may explain why someone chose a target, but it does not outweigh conduct that creates serious security risks.

The more an intrusion demonstrates planning, persistence and technical escalation, the less weight a curiosity claim is likely to carry. A person who continues exploring after recognising the sensitivity of the systems, repeatedly returns to them, acquires privileged access or copies information has made a series of deliberate choices that extend well beyond accidental discovery.

UK prosecution guidance reflects this broader assessment. Relevant considerations include the sophistication of the offending, efforts to conceal identity, financial, commercial or reputational damage, harm to victims and wider [public-interest]({{ 'public-interest/' | relative_url }}) factors. Although the absence of financial motivation may remove one aggravating feature, it does not eliminate others arising from the scale, persistence or impact of the intrusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/computer-misuse-act" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cps.gov.uk">[Crown Prosecution Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cps.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Crown Prosecution Service Computer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crown Prosecution ServiceComputer Misuse Act &#124; The Crown Prosecution ServiceAugust 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 2023</span></span></span>

## The broader lesson from the McKinnon case

The McKinnon case is often remembered because of its unusual claimed motivation, but its legal significance lies in demonstrating that criminal responsibility depends far more on the nature of the intrusion than on the explanation offered for it.

A brief, limited and responsibly reported security discovery presents a very different picture from prolonged unauthorised access to sensitive government systems. Duration, repeated access, privileged control, [military targets]({{ 'military-targets/' | relative_url }}), copied data, concealment and operational disruption all increase the potential harm and therefore the seriousness with which authorities are likely to view the conduct. Even where curiosity is genuine and financial gain is absent, these aggravating factors can outweigh the claimed motive when assessing criminal liability and sentencing.
