## Introduction

One of the most persistent features of the Gary McKinnon story is not a confirmed discovery but an interpretation. McKinnon said he searched US military and NASA systems because he expected evidence of hidden UFO programmes. When he later described encountering unusual file names, unfamiliar terminology and references to ships that did not appear in public records, many UFO enthusiasts treated those fragments as confirmation that they had found the long-sought proof of a secret space programme. Yet none of the key files he described has been independently authenticated or released for public examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.the-independent.com/news/science/gary-mckinnon-inside-the-head-of-a-super-hacker-6095677.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: the-independent.com">[The Independent]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">the-independent.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The IndependentGary McKinnon: Inside the head of a super hacker &#124; The Independent &#124; The IndependentJuly 11, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 11, 2006</span></span></span>

This illustrates a broader mechanism behind UFO-related intrusions: specialised military records are often difficult for outsiders to interpret. Classified organisations routinely use technical jargon, abbreviations, administrative labels and [code names]({{ 'code-names/' | relative_url }}) that can appear extraordinary when stripped of their operational context. For someone already convinced that governments conceal extraterrestrial technology, ambiguity can become evidence rather than uncertainty.

## Why military language is easy to misread

Military and defence organisations generate enormous quantities of records that are designed for internal administration rather than public understanding. Personnel databases, logistics spreadsheets, procurement systems and operational planning documents frequently contain terminology that assumes readers already understand the organisational context.

Several characteristics make these records especially vulnerable to misinterpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Administrative labels without explanation.** Internal database fields often use shorthand that makes sense only within one department.
* **Code names and project titles.** Many defence projects deliberately employ obscure or misleading names that reveal nothing about their actual purpose.
* **Incomplete records.** Viewing only a single spreadsheet, directory or database entry rarely reveals what a programme actually concerns.
* **Compartmentalisation.** Sensitive organisations separate information across different systems, meaning individual files often appear cryptic in isolation.

</div>

Someone browsing such systems without authorisation encounters disconnected fragments rather than complete narratives. The temptation is to fill the missing context with prior expectations.

## How expectations shape interpretation

Confirmation bias is the tendency to interpret new information in ways that reinforce existing beliefs while discounting alternative explanations. Psychological research has repeatedly shown that ambiguous evidence is especially likely to be interpreted according to prior expectations rather than neutral analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4328" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Opinion Dynamics with Confirmation Bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Opinion Dynamics with Confirmation BiasNovember 16, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 16, 2014</span></span></span>

Within UFO [disclosure culture]({{ 'disclosure-culture/' | relative_url }}), this bias has particular force because believers often begin with several assumptions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* governments possess hidden knowledge about non-human intelligence;
* evidence has been deliberately compartmentalised;
* official denials are themselves part of the concealment.

</div>

Under those assumptions, ordinary uncertainty acquires a very different meaning. An unexplained spreadsheet title is not simply unexplained—it becomes a possible glimpse behind the cover-up. Missing context is interpreted not as a limitation of the evidence but as proof that secrecy is working.

This does not require deliberate deception. People can sincerely interpret the same ambiguous document in radically different ways depending upon what they already expect to find.

## The "Non-Terrestrial Officers" example

McKinnon's most famous claim concerned an alleged spreadsheet headed "Non-Terrestrial Officers". He interpreted the phrase as referring to personnel associated with a covert off-world programme, particularly because he also reported seeing references to ship-to-ship transfers involving vessels unfamiliar to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.welivesecurity.com/2015/12/08/gary-mckinnon-reveals-detail-on-nasa-data-breach-and-extraterrestrial-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: welivesecurity.com">[welivesecurity.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">welivesecurity.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon reveals detail on NASA data breach and ‘extraterrestrial life’</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary Mc Kinnon reveals detail on NASA data breach and ‘extraterrestrial life’</span></span></span>

The wording, however, illustrates precisely why context matters.

Outside readers naturally interpret "non-terrestrial" in its everyday sense of "not from Earth". Within military or aerospace organisations, similar terminology could potentially describe numerous Earth-based concepts, including assets located beyond Earth's surface, orbital operations, satellite support, administrative categories or internal database conventions. Without the surrounding documentation, database definitions or system architecture, the phrase alone cannot establish what the file actually described.

An equally important limitation is provenance. No independently verified copy of the spreadsheet has ever been produced publicly. McKinnon has consistently maintained that he saw the file, but investigators, journalists and researchers have not been able to authenticate it independently because the alleged document was never preserved for external examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thegalacticmind.com/the-ufo-hacker-gary-mckinnon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thegalacticmind.com">[The Galactic Mind]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thegalacticmind.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Galactic Mind Gary Mc Kinnon and the Search Inside the Machine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Galactic MindGary McKinnon and the Search Inside the MachineApril 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 13, 2026</span></span></span>

Consequently, the spreadsheet functions more as testimony than as documentary evidence.

## Why isolated files rarely verify extraordinary claims

A recurring misconception in discussions of classified information is that any unusual document automatically validates an extraordinary hypothesis.

In reality, verification requires considerably more than a striking title.

For an internal military record to support claims about hidden UFO programmes, investigators would ordinarily need:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* authenticated copies of the original documents;
* clear provenance showing where and when they originated;
* surrounding documentation explaining terminology;
* corroboration from independent records;
* evidence that alternative administrative explanations have been ruled out.

</div>

Without those elements, isolated file names remain ambiguous observations rather than verified discoveries.

This distinction is especially important because classified organisations routinely generate terminology that sounds remarkable outside its intended environment. Intelligence history contains numerous examples of mundane projects carrying dramatic code names and highly sensitive programmes disguised under deliberately opaque labels.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_ufo_disclosure_hacke_1d428a_ambiguous_files_conf_27bf05-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Confirmation Bias illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_ufo_disclosure_hacke_1d428a_ambiguous_files_conf_27bf05-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_ufo_disclosure_hacke_1d428a_ambiguous_files_conf_27bf05-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## Secrecy itself can strengthen belief

Ironically, secrecy can amplify confirmation bias even when no extraordinary evidence exists.

Restricted computer systems contain many inaccessible files. Outsiders cannot easily determine whether an unfamiliar entry refers to logistics, accounting, software maintenance or classified operations. When information is incomplete, imagination often fills the gaps.

For believers already convinced that governments conceal UFO programmes, several features of classified systems reinforce existing expectations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* inaccessible files imply hidden knowledge;
* technical jargon appears deliberately cryptic;
* missing documentation suggests compartmentalisation;
* unexplained terminology becomes consistent with long-standing disclosure narratives.

</div>

The absence of complete evidence therefore does not necessarily weaken belief. Instead, ambiguity itself becomes part of the supporting narrative.

## What later investigations suggest

Subsequent reporting has complicated assumptions that unusual military terminology necessarily points towards extraterrestrial programmes. Investigations by the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) have found that decades of secrecy, compartmentalisation and, in some historical cases, deliberate internal disinformation contributed to UFO mythology surrounding genuine classified defence activities rather than confirmed alien technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/ufo-us-disinformation-45376f7e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">military deliberately spread disinformation about UFOs, contributing to decades of conspiracy theories. The probe, prompted by congressio...</span></span></span>

Those findings do not explain every individual claim made by McKinnon, nor do they prove what any specific file contained. They do, however, demonstrate that secret military environments naturally generate misunderstood documents, rumours and specialised language. In some historical cases, officials even encouraged misleading interpretations to shield sensitive terrestrial programmes.

Viewed in that broader context, McKinnon's account illustrates a cognitive mechanism more than a confirmed discovery. A person searching specifically for evidence of hidden UFO programmes encountered unfamiliar military terminology, interpreted it through an existing belief system and reported findings that remain impossible to verify independently. The episode shows how ambiguous records, incomplete context and strong prior expectations can combine to produce enduring claims that feel persuasive despite lacking the documentary evidence needed for confirmation.
