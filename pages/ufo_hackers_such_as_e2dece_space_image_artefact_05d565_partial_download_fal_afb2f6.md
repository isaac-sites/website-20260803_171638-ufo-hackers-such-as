## Introduction

An incomplete image download can make an ordinary space photograph appear to contain a sharply bounded, artificial object even when no such object exists. This is particularly relevant to discussions surrounding claims such as Gary McKinnon's description of a [NASA image]({{ 'nasa-image/' | relative_url }}) viewed over a slow remote connection. When an image is displayed before all of its data have arrived, or when packets are missing or corrupted, the decoding software may fill gaps with incorrect values, shift image blocks, truncate the frame or display only a coarse preview. The resulting picture can contain smooth outlines, abrupt geometric edges and isolated bright regions that resemble solid spacecraft, even though they are simply consequences of incomplete image reconstruction rather than features in the original scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia22929-opportunitys-last-message/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jpl.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jpl.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)Opportunity&#x27;s Last Message &#124; NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)March 12, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 12, 2019</span></span></span>

## How Missing Packets Disrupt Image Decoding

Digital space images are rarely transmitted as a single uninterrupted picture. Instead, they are broken into packets or compressed segments that are sent sequentially and later reconstructed. If some packets fail to arrive or become corrupted, the decoder must either stop, ignore the damaged section or attempt to reconstruct the image from incomplete information. The visual result depends on the image format, compression method and viewing software.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pds.nasa.gov/datastandards/documents/dd/all/current/ch43s248.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pds.nasa.gov">[Planetary Data System]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pds.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Planetary Data Systemsegment_status in Image_​Compression_​Segment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planetary Data Systemsegment_status in Image_​Compression_​Segment</span></span></span>

Typical effects include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* abrupt rectangular gaps where data are missing;
* shifted image blocks that no longer line up correctly;
* incorrect brightness or colour in part of the image;
* duplicated or misplaced structures;
* truncated images ending in solid black or random-looking regions.

</div>

When these defects occur against the dark background of space, they can create surprisingly convincing silhouettes. A bright astronomical feature or instrument reflection may become isolated inside a sharply bounded corrupted region, giving the impression of a smooth metallic craft rather than a decoding failure. Modern studies of corrupted JPEG bitstreams demonstrate that transmission errors can produce block shifts, colour distortions and displaced image content that are unrelated to the original scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06976" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bitstream-Corrupted JPEG Images are Restorable: Two-stage Compensation and Alignment Framework for Image RestorationApril 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 14, 2023</span></span></span>

NASA missions have documented comparable effects. The Cassini Imaging Science Subsystem user guide explains that dropped packets and memory errors can produce truncated lossy images, missing data and corrupted image lines when transmission errors occur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pds-imaging.jpl.nasa.gov/documentation/iss_data_user_guide_180916.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pds-imaging.jpl.nasa.gov">[pds-imaging.jpl.nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pds-imaging.jpl.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cassini Imaging Science Subsystem (ISS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Low-Resolution Previews Exaggerate False Shapes

The illusion becomes stronger when the viewer sees only a low-resolution or reduced-colour version of an image.

Many spacecraft first provide quick-look or preview products before the complete science-quality data are available. These previews often use stronger compression, fewer grey levels and lower spatial resolution so they can be transmitted rapidly. Fine texture disappears, while compression smooths boundaries into apparently coherent forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/multimedia/raw-images-faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Raw Images FAQ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceRaw Images FAQ - NASA ScienceNovember 2, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2, 2024</span></span></span>

If an incomplete download is viewed at the same time, several effects combine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* missing detail prevents the eye from recognising familiar astronomical structures;
* compression smooths irregular edges into apparently engineered curves;
* isolated bright regions become visually detached from their surroundings;
* the brain naturally groups disconnected patches into complete objects.

</div>

Human visual perception strongly favours recognising meaningful shapes, especially under conditions of incomplete information. A partially decoded image therefore encourages pareidolia—the tendency to perceive recognisable objects where none exist—while the technical artefacts provide the raw material for that interpretation.

NASA's STEREO mission illustrates a related issue. Its publicly available "beacon" images are intentionally highly compressed to provide rapid space-weather monitoring, and are later replaced with higher-quality science data. Features that appear unusual in the preview images often become ordinary detector artefacts or optical effects once the complete dataset is available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov/artifacts/wheel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov">[STEREO Science Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">STEREO Science Center</span></span></span>

## Real Examples of Incomplete Space Images

Space missions occasionally publish incomplete images precisely because communication ended before transmission finished.

One well-documented example is the final image returned by NASA's Opportunity rover during the 2018 Martian dust storm. The transmission stopped before the full frame arrived, leaving part of the image represented only by black pixels. The remaining visible region contains increased noise because the scene itself was extremely dark. No one interprets the black region as a physical object because mission documentation records that the transmission ended prematurely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia22929-opportunitys-last-message/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jpl.nasa.gov">[NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jpl.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)Opportunity&#x27;s Last Message &#124; NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)March 12, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 12, 2019</span></span></span>

Other missions have experienced different forms of image corruption:

* The STEREO spacecraft has occasionally produced corrupted images in which data from different telescopes became mixed, creating composite scenes that never existed optically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov/artifacts/artifacts_corrupted.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov">[STEREO Science Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">STEREO Science Center</span></span></span>
* NASA's Galileo mission returned scrambled Io images after radiation damage affected detector readout, producing a double-exposure appearance that engineers later reconstructed mathematically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/reconstruction-of-scrambled-io-images/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Reconstruction of Scrambled Io Images</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceReconstruction of Scrambled Io Images - NASA ScienceDecember 17, 1999...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 17, 1999</span></span></span>

## What Metadata Is Needed to Verify the Original Scene

A single displayed image is rarely sufficient to determine whether an apparent object is genuine. Verification depends on the accompanying technical information.

Useful metadata include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the original filename and observation identifier;
* acquisition time and spacecraft instrument;
* compression format and processing level;
* transmission status, including whether packets were missing;
* checksum or integrity verification;
* availability of the original uncompressed data;
* adjacent frames from the same observation sequence.

</div>

Mission archives routinely preserve these details because they allow engineers and scientists to distinguish genuine celestial features from transmission errors, detector artefacts or incomplete processing. Without this information, later investigators cannot reliably reconstruct what the viewer actually saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/how-are-webbs-full-color-images-made/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science How Are Webb’s Full-Color Images Made?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceHow Are Webb’s Full-Color Images Made? - NASA ScienceSeptember 3, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 3, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why This Matters for UFO Image Claims

Within discussions of alleged spacecraft visible in NASA imagery, incomplete downloads represent a plausible technical mechanism that should be excluded before more extraordinary explanations are considered.

In Gary McKinnon's account, the reported image was viewed remotely over a slow connection using reduced display settings, and no original file, screenshot or observation metadata were preserved. Under those circumstances, it is impossible to determine whether the apparent smooth, structured object reflected a genuine astronomical scene, an ordinary satellite, an optical artefact, a corrupted transmission or an incompletely rendered image. The absence of the original data means none of these possibilities can now be tested directly.

The broader lesson is not that every unusual space image results from partial downloads, but that interrupted transfers, compressed previews and incomplete decoding are well-understood sources of false visual structure. Before an apparent object is treated as evidence of an unknown spacecraft, investigators need the complete image, its metadata and, ideally, the original mission data products to establish what the camera actually recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov/artifacts/artifacts_corrupted.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stereo-ssc.nascom.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">STEREO Science Center</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">STEREO Science Center</span></span></span>
