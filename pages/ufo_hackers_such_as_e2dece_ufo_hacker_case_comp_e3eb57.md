## Introduction

All three entered poorly secured American government or military networks, and all became associated with searches for hidden information. Yet they were not one organised movement, did not attack the same systems at the same time, and did not produce equivalent claims about what they found.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_ufo_hacker_case_comp_e3eb57-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Case Comparison" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Bevan and Pryce belonged to the earlier, mid-1990s world of teenage and young-adult network exploration. Their activity became a landmark security incident because compromised US Air Force computers were used to reach numerous other systems. McKinnon acted alone several years later, expressly presenting his intrusions as an investigation of UFO secrecy and suppressed technology. Pryce was convicted and fined; the case against Bevan was abandoned; McKinnon spent a decade resisting extradition before the British government stopped the process on [human-rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}) grounds. Those distinctions matter because later retellings frequently merge three separate histories into a single story of hackers uncovering alien secrets.

## Shared motives and methods

The strongest connection between the three cases is not evidence of a common conspiracy but a combination of curiosity, distrust of official secrecy and exceptionally weak network security.

McKinnon, using the name “Solo”, admitted obtaining [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) from his London home to American military and NASA computers in 2001 and 2002. The United States alleged that he reached 97 government machines. He said he was looking for evidence that officials had concealed UFO information, antigravity research and technologies capable of providing abundant energy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — between 1 February 2001 and 19 March 2002 he had gaine...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 2001</span></span></span>

Bevan, known online as “Kuji”, also said that an interest in UFO secrecy influenced his activity. Contemporary and retrospective accounts associate him with attempts to enter NASA, US Air Force and defence-related systems while seeking information about advanced aircraft and alleged government cover-ups. This makes Bevan the closest genuine predecessor to McKinnon in terms of stated motive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.isc2.org/Insights/2024/09/CISSP30-30-Years-After-2-Kids-Broke-into-the-Air-Force" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isc2.org">[ISC2]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isc2.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CISSP30 30 Years After 2 Kids Broke into the Air Force</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISC2#CISSP30: 30 Years After Two Kids Broke into the Air Force12 Sept 2024 — In 1994, teenager Richard Pryce, known as “Datastream Cowboy...</span></span></span>

Pryce, whose handle was “Datastream Cowboy”, is regularly included in the same category because he worked within the same hacking milieu as Bevan and penetrated some of the same military infrastructure. Some later narrative accounts also describe him as interested in UFO material. Nevertheless, the official and contemporary record concentrates much more heavily on his repeated access to military computers than on a clearly documented personal quest for extraterrestrial evidence. It is therefore safer to treat Pryce’s UFO connection as less firmly established than those of Bevan and McKinnon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trulyadventure.us/the-hacker" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trulyadventure.us">[Truly*Adventurous]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trulyadventure.us</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truly*AdventurousThe HackerBut it wasn&#x27;t Mathew Bevan, aka Kuji. Their methods reflected the insecure early internet rather than sophisticated intelligence capabilities. The [Rome Laboratory]({{ 'rome-laboratory/' | relative_url }}) intruders exploited vulnerable systems, moved through interconnected networks and used compromised machines as stepping stones to further targets. McKinnon similarly described finding machines protected by weak, default or absent passwords and searching them remotely over an ordinary domestic connection. In both periods, mundane security failures allowed outsiders to appear far more powerful than their equipment or resources would otherwise suggest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/1998/January%201998/0198cyber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineWar in Cyberspaceby JT Correll · 1998 · Cited by 11 — Using a variety of techniques, investigators learned tha...</span></span></span>

The similarity should not be overstated, however. Bevan and Pryce operated during the same 1994 intrusion campaign and exchanged knowledge within the wider hacker scene. McKinnon acted later and independently. There is no credible evidence that he was recruited, directed or technically assisted by either man.

## Different targets and discoveries

The greatest difference lies in what each case actually established. Bevan and Pryce became historically significant because investigators documented a sprawling network-security breach. McKinnon became culturally significant because of what he later said he had seen.

### Rome Laboratory and the wider intrusion chain

The Bevan–Pryce episode centred on the US Air Force’s Rome Laboratory in New York, then a major military research facility. The documented attack began in March 1994 and continued for 26 days before it was contained. Air Force accounts recorded approximately 150 intrusions, complete access to around 30 systems and attempts to reach roughly 100 additional targets, including NASA facilities and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/1998/January%201998/0198cyber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineWar in Cyberspaceby JT Correll · 1998 · Cited by 11 — Using a variety of techniques, investigators learned tha...</span></span></span>

Pryce was identified as the 16-year-old north London hacker behind Datastream Cowboy. Investigators linked activity from his telephone line with intrusions at Rome Laboratory, while his online communications revealed an enthusiasm for attacking military “.mil” systems. He was arrested in May 1994 while using a relatively ordinary home computer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/1998/January%201998/0198cyber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineWar in Cyberspaceby JT Correll · 1998 · Cited by 11 — Using a variety of techniques, investigators learned tha...</span></span></span>

Bevan was several years older and regarded by investigators as the more technically capable of the pair. He remained unidentified for longer and was not arrested until June 1996. Accounts of the case describe Pryce as following routes or techniques associated with Kuji, although the available evidence does not justify treating every intrusion by one man as a jointly planned act by both.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trulyadventure.us/the-hacker" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trulyadventure.us">[Truly*Adventurous]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trulyadventure.us</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truly*AdventurousThe HackerBut it wasn&#x27;t Mathew Bevan, aka Kuji. It was Richard Pryce, 16, the young man behind the handle Datastream Cow...</span></span></span>

The incident’s most alarming episode involved access, through a Rome Laboratory system, to the Korean Atomic Research Institute. Investigators initially feared that the organisation might be in North Korea and that activity apparently originating from a US military computer could be interpreted as American espionage. It was subsequently established that the institute was South Korean. The frequently repeated claim that the hackers “almost started the Third World War” is therefore an exaggerated summary of a genuine investigative concern, not evidence that war was actually imminent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/1998/January%201998/0198cyber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineWar in Cyberspaceby JT Correll · 1998 · Cited by 11 — Using a variety of techniques, investigators learned tha...</span></span></span>

No authenticated UFO document or extraordinary technological discovery emerged from the prosecutions. Bevan’s search motive is relevant to understanding why he selected certain targets, but the enduring evidence from the case consists of logs, investigative records and the exposure of serious network weaknesses—not recovered proof of extraterrestrial activity.

### McKinnon’s remembered findings

McKinnon’s targets overlapped broadly with those of the earlier hackers—NASA and branches of the US military—but his alleged activity was more extensive in duration and became tied to specific discovery claims. The extradition proceedings stated that he accessed 97 government computers between 1 February 2001 and 19 [March 2002]({{ 'march-2002/' | relative_url }}). US authorities alleged deletion of data, disruption and substantial financial losses; McKinnon admitted unauthorised access but disputed the characterisation of his purpose and the extent of the damage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — between 1 February 2001 and 19 March 2002 he had gaine...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 2001</span></span></span>

His best-known claims concerned an unusual image resembling a large craft, a document headed “Non-Terrestrial Officers” and references to ships or personnel that he could not reconcile with publicly known military structures. These assertions transformed his story from a computer-crime case into a central episode of modern UFO culture.

They are also evidentially weaker than the technical facts of the intrusions. McKinnon did not preserve the purported image, document or associated files in a form that could be independently authenticated. Investigators confirmed that he entered protected systems, but that does not independently confirm his interpretation of what appeared on them. The possibility of mundane military terminology, misunderstood data, incomplete context or mistaken memory therefore remains open.

This creates a sharp contrast. In the Rome Laboratory case, the extraordinary part was the demonstrable extent of the security failure, while the UFO motive remained secondary. In McKinnon’s case, the verified access provides a foundation for the story, but the alleged UFO discoveries depend largely on his later testimony.

## Three distinct legal outcomes

The three cases also ended very differently, even though each involved unauthorised access from Britain to American systems.

**Richard Pryce received a conviction and a modest domestic penalty.** In March 1997, after pleading guilty to 12 offences under the Computer Misuse Act, he was fined £1,200 and ordered to pay £250 in costs. He was 19 at sentencing but had been only 16 during the intrusions. The punishment now appears light beside later cybercrime sentences, reflecting both his youth and the comparatively early stage of British computer-crime enforcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irishtimes.com/news/1-200-fine-for-hacking-into-secrets-1.54802" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irishtimes.com">[The Irish Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irishtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Irish Times£1,200 fine for hacking into secrets – The Irish Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Irish Times£1,200 fine for hacking into secrets – The Irish Times</span></span></span>

**Mathew Bevan was formally acquitted after the prosecution offered no evidence.** He had been charged following his 1996 arrest, but in November 1997 the Crown Prosecution Service concluded that continuing the case was no longer in the public interest. The result was not a judicial finding that none of the alleged intrusions had occurred; rather, the prosecution chose not to proceed and a full acquittal was recorded. That distinction is sometimes lost when summaries simply state that he was “let off”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Bevan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mathew Bevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mathew Bevan</span></span></span>

**Gary McKinnon was neither extradited nor prosecuted in Britain.** The American extradition request produced years of litigation, during which British courts generally accepted that the legal requirements for extradition had been met. In October 2012, Home Secretary Theresa May nevertheless withdrew the extradition order because medical evidence indicated that removal would create a high risk of suicide and would therefore be incompatible with his human rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United...30 Jul 2008 — between 1 February 2001 and 19 March 2002 he had gaine...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 2001</span></span></span>

A subsequent British review concluded that McKinnon would face no further criminal action. Prosecutors cited difficulties in constructing a domestic case that reflected the full alleged conduct, including the location of witnesses and evidence in the United States. The decision did not authenticate his UFO claims or declare the intrusions lawful; it ended the realistic prospect of a trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/dec/14/gary-mckinnon-no-uk-charges" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

McKinnon’s outcome also had a much wider political impact. His case became part of a national dispute over the UK–US extradition relationship, prosecutorial pressure and the treatment of vulnerable defendants. Bevan and Pryce’s cases had highlighted deficiencies in computer security and digital investigation. McKinnon’s case eventually influenced debate about where cross-border offences should be tried and how human-rights considerations should constrain extradition.

## What the comparison changes

Treating McKinnon, Bevan and Pryce as interchangeable “UFO hackers” produces three misleading impressions.

First, it suggests that all three made comparable claims of discovering alien evidence. McKinnon offered detailed recollections of particular images and documents. Bevan openly connected his activities with a search for UFO information, but no equivalent body of claimed discoveries was established. Pryce’s association with the UFO theme is more indirect and less consistently documented.

Second, the label can obscure the actual cybersecurity importance of the Rome Laboratory affair. Bevan and Pryce demonstrated how an intruder could move through trusted networks, use a military computer as a launching point and create international confusion without possessing advanced hardware. The case was historically important even if every UFO-related interpretation is removed.

Third, their legal histories show that unauthorised access does not lead to a predictable outcome. Pryce’s guilty plea produced a fine. Bevan’s prosecution collapsed before trial. McKinnon faced the gravest threatened consequences and the longest legal struggle, yet ultimately received neither an American trial nor a British prosecution. Those differences resulted from age, evidence, charging decisions, cross-border [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}), the development of computer-crime law and, in McKinnon’s case, compelling medical and human-rights considerations.

The most defensible way to group the three men is therefore as figures from two generations of British intrusion into poorly protected US government networks, influenced to differing degrees by UFO secrecy narratives. Beyond that limited resemblance, their conduct, claims, evidential records and historical legacies remain markedly different.
