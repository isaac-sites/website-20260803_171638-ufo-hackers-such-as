## Introduction

Gary McKinnon’s case began with a series of unauthorised connections from London to poorly protected United States government computers and ended more than a decade later without a trial in either country. The formal US allegation covered access between **1 February 2001 and 19 [March 2002]({{ 'march-2002/' | relative_url }})** to **97 military and NASA computers**. Investigators said McKinnon used administrative accounts, installed remote-control software, copied password files and moved from one compromised network to another. McKinnon admitted gaining [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) but disputed the prosecution’s account of deliberate destruction, saying his main aim was to search for concealed UFO and advanced-technology material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

What followed was not a conventional hacking prosecution. US indictments in 2002 led to an extradition request in 2004, arrest under extradition law in 2005, repeated appeals, a major British debate over [mental health]({{ 'mental-health/' | relative_url }}) and US–UK legal arrangements, and the Home Secretary’s decision in October 2012 to stop his removal. British prosecutors then declined to bring a domestic case, leaving the factual disputes over damage and intent unresolved by a criminal trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

## Access between 2001 and 2002

The most reliable overall time window comes from the US extradition request later summarised by the House of Lords: McKinnon was alleged to have entered 97 US government computers between **1 February 2001 and 19 March 2002**. A Virginia indictment described a slightly narrower period, from March 2001 to March 2002, involving 92 military and NASA machines and six private-company computers. These figures are not necessarily contradictory: the 97-machine total used in the extradition proceedings referred to government systems, while particular indictments grouped the alleged conduct according to [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}) and charges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

According to the judicial account, McKinnon searched for Microsoft Windows systems that exposed network connections and administrative accounts. Once he obtained access, he installed the commercial remote-administration program **RemotelyAnywhere**, together with tools that enabled further scanning and helped conceal activity. The compromised computers could then be used as stepping stones into additional systems. US evidence presented in the extradition case said this process allowed him to scan more than 73,000 government computers for similar weaknesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

This method belonged to a particular moment in internet history. It did not depend on defeating a single heavily fortified central system. Instead, it exploited repeated weaknesses across numerous ordinary networked computers, including weak account protection, unnecessary exposed services and the ability to install legitimate remote-control software without prompt detection. The scale therefore came from repetition: identify a vulnerable machine, obtain administrative access, establish persistent remote control and use that position to look for further targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

McKinnon later connected his activity to a search for evidence of UFO secrecy, unconventional spacecraft and suppressed energy technology. That motivation explains why NASA and military systems were attractive to him, but it does not establish what each individual intrusion was intended to find. The indictments and extradition judgments chiefly document system access, installed software, copied credentials, deleted material and alleged disruption; they do not provide a day-by-day record of which UFO-related files he searched on every machine.

### April to September 2001: Naval Weapons Station Earle

The New Jersey indictment supplies the most detailed sequence for any single network. It alleged that on **7 April 2001**, McKinnon entered the network at Naval Weapons Station Earle, a facility responsible for supporting the US Navy’s Atlantic Fleet. The initial point of access was said to be the Port Services computer, which was used to monitor information about ships around the station’s pier complex, including their location, condition, staffing, readiness and resupply status. Prosecutors alleged that he installed RemotelyAnywhere on that computer and others connected to the same network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Between **18 and 21 June 2001**, the indictment said he repeatedly returned through the installed remote-access software and obtained approximately 950 passwords stored on network servers. This stage is important because it shows how the alleged campaign could persist over time: the April access was not described as a one-off visit but as the establishment of a route that could be reused months later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Prosecutors alleged another entry on **23 September 2001**, less than two weeks after the terrorist attacks of 11 September. During that intrusion, McKinnon was accused of deleting startup files and logs, compromising the network’s security and causing about $290,431 in damage. US officials said all 300 computers on the station network were effectively shut down for a week, with limited internal email for a further three weeks and full automated message routing and internet access restored only after roughly a month.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Naval Weapons Station Computers, Disabling Network After Sept. 11 (N...</span></span></span>

Those allegations became among the most serious elements of the case because of their timing and the station’s military function. McKinnon denied that he had intentionally caused the level of damage claimed by US authorities. As no criminal trial occurred, neither the prosecution’s full technical evidence nor the defence’s challenge to its damage calculations was tested before a jury.

### The wider military and NASA campaign

Beyond Earle, the Virginia case alleged access to computers belonging to the US Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA, including two machines at the Pentagon. Prosecutors said McKinnon copied password and other files, deleted accounts and system material, and installed both remote-administration and hacking tools. They also alleged that he used compromised computers to identify and enter additional targets, turning separate weak machines into a linked campaign.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

The House of Lords judgment recorded allegations that critical operating-system files were deleted from nine computers, contributing to the shutdown of the US Army’s [Military District]({{ 'network-shutdown/' | relative_url }}) of Washington network. That network served more than 2,000 users. The 2008 judgment described the outage as lasting 24 hours, while the original 2002 Justice Department announcement referred to loss of internet and email services for approximately 2,000 users for three days. The difference probably reflects distinct measures of disruption or later refinement of the evidence, but the surviving public records do not fully reconcile the two descriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

The same judgment also recorded the deletion of 2,455 user accounts from an Army computer that controlled network access, allegedly causing machines to reboot and become unusable. These claims formed part of the US portrayal of the activity as damaging interference with government operations rather than harmless browsing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

McKinnon’s own version separated unauthorised entry from malicious destruction. He acknowledged entering systems and searching them but rejected the image of himself as a saboteur. That distinction became central to public discussion, yet the legal record never resolved it. Extradition courts were deciding whether he could be sent to face prosecution, not determining whether every allegation in the indictments had been proved beyond reasonable doubt.

### March 2002: identification and the end of the access period

The extradition request fixed **19 March 2002** as the final date of the alleged intrusion period. On that day, British authorities acted on a US request for legal assistance, seized McKinnon’s home computer and interviewed him under caution. He was questioned again on **8 August 2002**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

## Charges and extradition proceedings

Federal grand juries brought separate cases in New Jersey and Virginia. The New Jersey indictment was returned on **31 October 2002** and concerned Naval Weapons Station Earle. On **12 November 2002**, a Virginia grand jury charged McKinnon with seven counts of computer fraud and related activity. The Virginia Justice Department announcement estimated damage at approximately $900,000 and stated that the United States intended to request extradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

Between **November 2002 and April 2003**, McKinnon’s lawyers and US prosecutors discussed a possible voluntary surrender and guilty plea. The House of Lords later examined these negotiations because McKinnon argued that the disparity between the proposed outcomes placed improper pressure on him not to contest extradition. According to the judgment, prosecutors indicated that cooperation might produce a sentence of about three to four years, with a relatively short initial period in the United States and possible transfer to Britain. If he resisted extradition, went to trial and was convicted, they suggested that the sentence might be eight to ten years or longer, with no prosecutorial support for repatriation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

McKinnon rejected the proposed agreement. US arrest warrants followed in August and September 2004, and the formal extradition request was submitted to the British government on **7 October 2004**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

He was arrested under the Extradition Act on **7 June 2005** and appeared at Bow Street Magistrates’ Court the next day. The extradition hearing began on 27 July 2005 and continued on several dates during February and April 2006. On **10 May 2006**, District Judge Nicholas Evans sent the case to the Home Secretary for a decision. Home Secretary John Reid ordered extradition on **4 July 2006**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

McKinnon appealed both the judicial and ministerial decisions. On **3 April 2007**, the Divisional Court dismissed his appeals, although it certified legal questions of wider public importance. The House of Lords granted permission to appeal on one question in October 2007: whether US plea negotiations, particularly the suggested consequences of resisting extradition, amounted to an abuse of process or an unjustified interference with his rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

On **30 July 2008**, the House of Lords unanimously dismissed the appeal. The Law Lords concluded that the discussions did not justify stopping the extradition proceedings. Their decision did not determine McKinnon’s guilt or validate every damage allegation; it held that the conduct complained of did not make the extradition process unlawful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

McKinnon then applied to the European Court of [Human Rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}). The court briefly prevented removal while considering his request but declined to maintain the interim restriction in August 2008.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2008/aug/28/hacking.security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianHacker Gary McKinnon loses appeal against extradition to...August 28, 2008 — 28 Aug 2008 — Last month, he lost his battle ag...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2008</span></span></span>

By this stage, however, the case had acquired a new medical dimension. McKinnon was diagnosed with Asperger’s syndrome shortly after the House of Lords decision, and his representatives submitted psychiatric evidence concerning depression and suicide risk. These arguments shifted the centre of the case away from plea bargaining and towards whether extradition would expose him to treatment or consequences incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vlex.co.uk/vid/r-mckinnon-v-secretary-793475865" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vlex.co.uk">[vLex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vlex.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">r mckinnon v secretary 793475865</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">r mckinnon v secretary 793475865</span></span></span>

On **23 January 2009**, the High Court permitted a further judicial-review challenge. On **31 July 2009**, it rejected his attempt to overturn the decision to proceed. The court acknowledged serious medical evidence and the risk that extradition, trial and imprisonment would damage his mental health, but held that the legal threshold for preventing removal had not then been met.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2009/jul/31/gary-mckinnon-hacker-aspergers-us" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Gary Mc Kinnon should be extradited, court rules</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Gary Mc Kinnon should be extradited, court rules</span></span></span>

The dispute nevertheless continued through further medical submissions, political campaigning and government review. In November 2010, the Home Office summarised a procedural history in which successive courts had permitted extradition but outstanding representations required consideration of whether removal would violate McKinnon’s human rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/latest-on-gary-mckinnon-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">latest on gary mckinnon case</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">latest on gary mckinnon case</span></span></span>

## How the case ended in Britain

The decisive change came on **16 October 2012**. Home Secretary Theresa May told the House of Commons that the ordinary statutory extradition process had ended and that the remaining issue was whether removal would breach McKinnon’s human rights. After reviewing submissions from clinicians, obtaining independent medical advice and taking legal advice, she concluded that extradition created such a high risk of suicide that it would be incompatible with his rights. She therefore withdrew the extradition order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UK...</span></span></span>

May’s decision did not declare McKinnon innocent and did not reject the seriousness of the alleged offences. It was an exceptional decision based on his health and the predicted consequences of removal. At the same time, she announced plans for a “forum bar”, giving British courts a clearer role in deciding whether a case capable of prosecution in more than one country should be tried in the United Kingdom rather than abroad. The McKinnon controversy had therefore become part of a wider reconsideration of extradition procedure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UK...</span></span></span>

The remaining question was whether British authorities would prosecute him domestically. On **14 December 2012**, the Crown Prosecution Service and Metropolitan Police announced that no further criminal action would be taken in Britain. They cited the practical difficulty of transferring evidence and witnesses from the United States, the age and complexity of the material, and the assessment that a conviction in a British court was unlikely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/dec/14/gary-mckinnon-no-uk-charges" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gary mckinnon no uk charges</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianGary McKinnon will face no charges in UK14 Dec 2012 — No further legal action will be taken in Britain against the computer h...</span></span></span>

That decision brought the case to an unusual conclusion. McKinnon had admitted unauthorised access, and US prosecutors had obtained indictments describing serious disruption, yet no court conducted a complete criminal trial. As a result, the chronology is much firmer than the final factual assessment. The dates of access, investigation, indictment and extradition litigation are well documented; the exact extent of intentional damage, the calculation of losses and the interpretation of individual system actions remain allegations contested by McKinnon rather than findings reached after trial.

      });

          event.stopPropagation();

      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);

    });
