## Introduction

British UFO-minded hackers such as Gary McKinnon and, to a lesser extent, [Mathew Bevan]({{ 'mathew-bevan/' | relative_url }}) did not choose American military and space systems at random. Their attention focused on the United States because it occupied a unique place in UFO folklore, Cold War secrecy and advanced aerospace research. In the minds of these hackers, if any government possessed hidden evidence of unidentified flying objects, recovered technology or classified propulsion research, it would most likely be the United States—and particularly organisations such as NASA, the US Air Force and the Department of Defense. At the same time, the rapid expansion of internet-connected [government networks]({{ 'network-blind-spots/' | relative_url }}) during the 1990s and early 2000s created opportunities that were far more accessible than many outsiders imagined. The combination of powerful mythology and unexpectedly weak cyber security made American systems unusually attractive targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-autistic-hacker" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spectrum.ieee.org">[ieee.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spectrum.ieee.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spectrum Gary Mc Kinnon: The Autistic Hacker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IEEE SpectrumGary McKinnon: The Autistic Hacker - IEEE SpectrumJune 27, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2011</span></span></span>

## How UFO folklore concentrated attention on US institutions

Long before internet hacking became widespread, UFO culture had become heavily centred on American military and scientific institutions. Stories surrounding Roswell, Area 51, classified Air Force projects, secret aerospace programmes and alleged government cover-ups dominated books, documentaries and conference discussions throughout the 1980s and 1990s.

For British enthusiasts, this created a straightforward assumption: if documentary proof existed anywhere, it would probably be inside American government computers rather than British ones. NASA occupied a particularly important symbolic position because of its role in space exploration, satellite imagery and astronaut missions, while the US military represented the institutions believed to possess the highest levels of classified aerospace information.

Gary McKinnon repeatedly explained that his motivation came from searching for evidence supporting claims made by the [Disclosure Project]({{ 'disclosure-influence/' | relative_url }}) and other UFO researchers. According to his own account, he expected military and NASA systems to contain evidence of advanced propulsion, suppressed energy technologies or non-human spacecraft rather than conventional intelligence secrets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_McKinnon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary Mc Kinnon</span></span></span>

Mathew Bevan's reported motivation reflected a similar belief. Contemporary investigators and later reporting described him as attempting to find evidence supporting UFO conspiracy theories while exploring American military networks, reinforcing the pattern that UFO-focused British hackers looked westward rather than towards domestic government systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Bevan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mathew Bevan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mathew Bevan</span></span></span>

## Why military and NASA networks seemed technically reachable

The mythology alone does not explain why these systems became actual hacking targets. Just as important was the perception—and often the reality—that many publicly reachable American government computers were surprisingly insecure.

During the late 1990s and early 2000s:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* numerous administrative systems remained connected to the internet;
* default or blank passwords were still common;
* remote administration software was widely deployed;
* trust relationships between different government networks sometimes allowed movement from less sensitive systems towards more valuable ones.

</div>

McKinnon later described beginning with relatively ordinary support and logistics systems rather than immediately attacking highly classified computers. Once inside a trusted network, he attempted to move progressively towards systems that appeared more sensitive. This approach relied less on sophisticated hacking techniques than on exploiting weak authentication and poor security practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2007/apr/03/politics.usa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

One of the striking features of the McKinnon case is that many accounts—including his own—emphasise how little technical sophistication was required. He claimed to have scanned thousands of government machines automatically, discovering administrators who had left passwords blank or unchanged. Whether every detail of his description is accurate, official court documents and the US indictment likewise describe extensive [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) obtained across Army, Navy, Air Force, NASA and Department of Defense systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

## Why American institutions appeared more valuable than British ones

British UFO enthusiasts generally regarded American institutions as possessing three characteristics that made them uniquely interesting.

First, the United States maintained the world's largest defence establishment and aerospace research infrastructure. If secret aircraft or recovered technologies existed, American agencies appeared more likely than Britain's to possess them.

Second, NASA offered a civilian image while simultaneously operating alongside military and intelligence programmes. For conspiracy theorists, this combination encouraged speculation that public science concealed classified discoveries.

Third, American government networks were numerous and geographically distributed. Thousands of interconnected systems supporting logistics, research, administration and military operations created a much larger attack surface than that available within British government infrastructure. Even if only peripheral systems were accessible, hackers believed they might eventually lead towards more interesting material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2007/apr/03/politics.usa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## How each successful login reinforced the search

An important psychological feature of these cases was the feedback loop created by successful intrusions.

Every successful login appeared to confirm two beliefs simultaneously:

* that American systems really did contain hidden material worth pursuing;
* that further exploration might eventually uncover decisive evidence.

Finding poorly protected government computers could itself seem extraordinary to someone already convinced that major secrets existed behind official walls. Instead of treating easy access as evidence of ordinary cyber security failures, a determined UFO believer could interpret each breakthrough as one step closer to discovering concealed programmes.

McKinnon later described his search as becoming increasingly obsessive. He reported moving from one system to another in the hope of finding stronger evidence, explaining that each discovery encouraged him to continue searching rather than stop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-autistic-hacker" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spectrum.ieee.org">[IEEE Spectrum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spectrum.ieee.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spectrum Gary Mc Kinnon: The Autistic Hacker</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IEEE SpectrumGary McKinnon: The Autistic Hacker - IEEE SpectrumJune 27, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2011</span></span></span>

## The gap between expectations and verified evidence

Although McKinnon has consistently claimed that he observed unusual files—including references to "non-terrestrial officers" and an image he believed showed an unidentified craft—these claims have never been independently verified. No publicly released forensic evidence has confirmed that such documents represented extraterrestrial technology or hidden space programmes. The claims therefore remain personal testimony rather than established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_McKinnon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary Mc Kinnon</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because it explains why American systems remained attractive despite the lack of confirmed discoveries. For believers, the possibility that proof existed somewhere within an immense military bureaucracy justified continued searching. For investigators, however, the lasting significance of these cases lay less in what the hackers claimed to have found than in what their intrusions revealed about cyber security weaknesses across major US government networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 2002)...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2002</span></span></span>

## American targets became symbols as much as objectives

The enduring association between British UFO hackers and American defence systems reflects the convergence of culture and technology rather than either factor alone. UFO folklore directed attention towards US military and space agencies because they dominated popular narratives about secrecy and advanced aerospace research. At the same time, weaknesses in internet-connected government systems made those institutions appear reachable rather than untouchable.

For hackers such as Gary McKinnon, this combination created a powerful incentive. American defence and NASA networks were not merely technical targets—they represented the places where decades of UFO mythology suggested the answers would be found, while their unexpectedly weak security made that search appear achievable.
