## Introduction

Gary McKinnon’s extradition battle was never simply about whether he had accessed United States government computers without authorisation. He admitted the [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}). The central legal dispute became whether a British citizen who carried out the alleged offences from London should be sent to the United States for trial. American prosecutors argued that the computers targeted, the alleged victims, the claimed financial losses and the national security implications all lay within US [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}), making prosecution in the United States the appropriate course. McKinnon and his supporters, by contrast, argued that the conduct occurred in the UK, that any prosecution could have taken place in Britain, and that extradition exposed him to disproportionate punishment and intense pressure to accept a plea agreement. These competing views shaped one of the UK's most prominent extradition cases.

## The legal case for US prosecution

The United States built its extradition request on the principle that crimes can be prosecuted where their principal effects are felt, even if the accused acted from another country. According to the indictments returned in New Jersey and the Eastern District of Virginia, McKinnon unlawfully accessed US Army, Navy, Air Force, Department of Defense and NASA computers, causing damage to systems located in the United States. Prosecutors alleged that these intrusions disrupted military networks, affected computers used for national defence and imposed hundreds of thousands of dollars in recovery costs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

From the US perspective, several factors supported extradition:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The alleged victims were American government agencies and organisations.
* The affected computer systems were physically located in the United States.
* Grand juries in two federal districts had returned criminal indictments.
* The alleged offences fell within the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and related federal criminal statutes.
* The United States maintained that the seriousness of the alleged damage justified prosecution before an American court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

US authorities therefore argued that Britain should honour its extradition treaty obligations by surrendering McKinnon to stand trial where the alleged harm occurred.

An additional practical consideration was that much of the documentary and technical evidence, along with many witnesses responsible for administering the affected networks, were located in the United States. Trying the case there would avoid transporting extensive evidence across jurisdictions.

## Why Britain became the centre of the dispute

McKinnon never travelled to the United States while carrying out the intrusions. He operated from London using his home computer and internet connection. That fact became the foundation of the defence argument that any criminal proceedings should occur in Britain rather than overseas.

Supporters questioned why UK prosecutors did not bring domestic computer misuse charges instead. At the time, the Extradition Act 2003 did not include the later "forum bar", which now allows British courts in some circumstances to refuse extradition where prosecution in the UK would better serve the interests of justice. During McKinnon's lengthy legal battle, that safeguard was unavailable, meaning the courts largely considered whether the statutory requirements for extradition had been met rather than choosing the most appropriate venue for trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld201415/ldselect/ldextradition/126/12620.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Extradition: UK law and practice - Select Committee on Extradition Law...</span></span></span>

The case therefore became a wider political debate about whether British citizens could be removed to face trial abroad for conduct carried out entirely within the UK.

## The disputed plea-bargain pressure

One of the most controversial features of the case involved discussions between US prosecutors and McKinnon's legal representatives before the formal extradition request.

According to the House of Lords judgment, prosecutors indicated that if McKinnon voluntarily travelled to the United States and pleaded guilty, they would support a substantially more favourable outcome than if he resisted extradition and contested the charges. The discussions reportedly covered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the charges likely to be pursued;
* expected sentencing ranges;
* whether prosecutors would support transferring him back to Britain to serve any sentence.

</div>

McKinnon's legal team argued that these negotiations amounted to improper pressure, effectively forcing him to choose between exercising his legal right to challenge extradition or accepting a guilty plea to avoid a potentially much harsher sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

American prosecutors regarded the discussions as ordinary plea bargaining, a routine and lawful feature of the US criminal justice system. British appellate courts ultimately accepted that plea negotiations of this type did not, by themselves, amount to an abuse of the extradition process. The House of Lords therefore rejected this ground of appeal, although it acknowledged that the issue raised an important question of public importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

The controversy nevertheless resonated in Britain because plea bargaining plays a much smaller role in English criminal procedure, leading many observers to view the pressure as unusually severe.

## How British courts initially ruled

The legal process consistently favoured extradition during its early stages.

In 2006, a district judge at Bow Street Magistrates' Court found that the statutory conditions for extradition had been met and referred the case to the Home Secretary, who ordered McKinnon's extradition. McKinnon appealed unsuccessfully to the High Court, which upheld both the extradition decision and the Home Secretary's order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vlex.co.uk/vid/mckinnon-v-united-states-793612009" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vlex.co.uk">[vLex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vlex.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">v Lex Mc Kinnon v United States of America</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McKinnon v United States of America - vLex United Kingdom...</span></span></span>

The House of Lords later dismissed his final appeal on the principal legal issues, including the challenge based on the plea-bargaining discussions. At that stage, the courts had concluded that the extradition request complied with UK extradition law and with the applicable provisions of the European Convention on [Human Rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

These decisions meant that, from a judicial standpoint, extradition remained lawful despite widespread public debate over fairness and proportionality.

## Why extradition ultimately did not happen

Although the United States maintained its request, the case took a different direction after extensive medical evidence was presented concerning McKinnon's Asperger's syndrome, depression and risk of suicide.

In October 2012, Home Secretary Theresa May concluded that extradition would create such a high risk that McKinnon would take his own life that surrendering him would breach his human rights. She therefore withdrew the extradition order, despite emphasising that he remained accused of serious offences. Her decision rested on exceptional medical and human-rights grounds rather than disagreement with the United States' jurisdiction or with the earlier court rulings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/gary-mckinnon-extradition-case-home-secretarys-statement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gary Mc Kinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon extradition case: Home Secretary&#x27;s statement - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

## Why the extradition dispute remains significant

The American request to extradite Gary McKinnon became far more than a dispute over computer hacking. It highlighted fundamental questions about cross-border cybercrime, including where internet offences should be tried, how extradition treaties should operate when conduct occurs remotely, and whether differences between legal systems can create unequal pressure on defendants.

For supporters of extradition, the case demonstrated that attacks on government systems should be prosecuted where the victims, evidence and alleged harm are located. For critics, it illustrated the potential imbalance faced by individuals accused of offences committed online from another country, particularly where the prospect of severe sentences and plea bargaining may influence decisions long before any trial begins. The debate helped shape wider discussion in the UK about extradition law and contributed to later reforms that gave British courts greater ability to consider whether domestic prosecution would be the more appropriate forum in future cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld201415/ldselect/ldextradition/126/12620.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Extradition: UK law and practice - Select Committee on Extradition Law...</span></span></span>
