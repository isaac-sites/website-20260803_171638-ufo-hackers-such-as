## Introduction

The alleged intrusion at **Naval Weapons Station (NWS) Earle on 7 April 2001** occupies a distinctive place in the case against Gary McKinnon because prosecutors did not present it as an isolated break-in. Instead, they argued that this first compromise created a **persistent foothold** that allowed him to return months later without repeating the original intrusion. According to the US indictment, the key step was the installation of commercially available remote-administration software, combined with the later theft of hundreds of stored passwords. This sequence became one of the clearest examples in the prosecution's narrative of how an initial compromise could be converted into continuing access rather than a one-time entry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Unlike broader allegations covering dozens of military and NASA systems, the Earle indictment described a detailed chronology: the first entry in April 2001, repeated returns in June, and another alleged intrusion in September by using the previously installed software. That chronology made Earle the prosecution's strongest illustration of persistence across time rather than simply repeated scanning of vulnerable computers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. ## Why the Port Services computer mattered

The April 2001 indictment identified the initial target as the **Port Services computer** at Naval Weapons Station Earle. Prosecutors described it as the primary system used to monitor the identity, location, physical condition, staffing, battle readiness and resupply status of Navy ships operating around the installation's pier complex. Because the machine occupied an operational role inside the base's network, compromising it allegedly offered access well beyond a single workstation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Naval Weapons Station Computers, Disabling Network After Sept. 11 (N...</span></span></span>

NWS Earle itself was responsible for supporting the US Navy's Atlantic Fleet through the movement of ammunition and supplies. For that reason, prosecutors treated the Port Services computer as strategically significant rather than merely another internet-connected Windows machine. The emphasis was not that the system contained classified UFO-related material, but that it served as an effective platform from which additional network access could be obtained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. The focus on this individual computer also distinguishes the Earle allegations from many other systems mentioned in the wider McKinnon case, where court documents identify compromised computers but provide far less detail about their operational function.

## Installing reusable remote access

According to the New Jersey indictment, the defining event during the April intrusion was the installation of **RemotelyAnywhere**, a legitimate commercial remote-administration product. Rather than alleging that McKinnon created custom malware, prosecutors argued that he installed standard administrative software capable of allowing internet-based control of the compromised computer. The same software was allegedly installed on other systems connected to the Earle network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. This distinction mattered because the prosecution's theory depended on **persistence** rather than repeated exploitation. Once RemotelyAnywhere had been installed, investigators alleged that it provided a mechanism for reconnecting directly to the compromised machines without repeating the original method of entry. House of Lords judgments summarising the extradition evidence likewise described McKinnon as installing unauthorised remote-access software that enabled later access while helping conceal his activity, together with additional tools for scanning and further compromise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMicrosoft Word - McKinnon _Appellant_ v Government of the United States of America _Respondents_ and another.doc...</span></span></span>

## Returning through the April foothold

The indictment alleges that the April installation was actively reused during **18–21 June 2001**. Rather than describing a fresh intrusion, prosecutors stated that McKinnon accessed the Port Services computer through the previously installed RemotelyAnywhere software. During these sessions he allegedly copied approximately **950 passwords** stored on connected server computers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. Those password files became an important part of the prosecution's reconstruction of events. ## How the April entry shaped later intrusions

The same indictment alleges that on **23 September 2001**, after the attacks of 11 September, McKinnon again entered the Earle network by using the previously installed RemotelyAnywhere software together with the passwords obtained earlier. Prosecutors argued that this was a continuation of the access first established in April rather than a completely separate breach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. US authorities further alleged that during this later intrusion he deleted operating-system files and logs, leaving parts of the network inoperable and requiring extensive recovery work. Those allegations became some of the most serious factual claims in the entire McKinnon prosecution because they connected the persistent access established months earlier with operational disruption after 11 September. McKinnon, however, consistently admitted [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) while disputing that he intentionally caused the damage alleged by US prosecutors. Because no US criminal trial ultimately took place, those competing accounts were never tested before a jury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeBritish National Charged with Hacking Into N.J. ## Why Earle became the prosecution's clearest persistence example

Across the broader McKinnon investigation, US authorities alleged repeated compromises of military and NASA systems through weak administrative security and the installation of remote-access software. Earle stands out because the charging documents provide a rare chronological chain linking **initial access, persistence, credential theft and later re-entry**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentMicrosoft Word - McKinnon _Appellant_ v Government of the United States of America _Respondents_ and another.doc...</span></span></span>

That sequence also illustrates a wider security lesson recognised in later court summaries. A successful compromise was treated not as the end of an intrusion but as the beginning of continued access. The alleged installation of legitimate administration software, followed by acquisition of stored credentials, meant that the first breach supposedly continued to generate opportunities months later. Within the overall timeline of McKinnon's alleged intrusions, this makes the April 2001 compromise at Naval Weapons Station Earle the prosecution's most detailed example of how one successful entry could become a lasting foothold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>
