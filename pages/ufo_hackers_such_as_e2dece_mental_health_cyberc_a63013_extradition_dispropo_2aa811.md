## Introduction

Mental illness does not automatically prevent extradition, nor does it excuse alleged criminal conduct. The legal question is narrower and more demanding: whether removing a particular person to face prosecution abroad would create a level of suffering so severe that extradition itself becomes a disproportionate and inhumane response. In cases such as Gary McKinnon's, the focus shifted away from the allegations and towards the foreseeable consequences of removal—separation from established support networks, disruption of psychiatric care, unfamiliar detention conditions and an exceptional risk of suicide. The issue is therefore not whether prosecution is justified, but whether extradition adds avoidable harm beyond that inherent in a criminal trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

## When does extradition become inhumane for vulnerable defendants?

The distinction between prosecution and extradition is crucial. Every criminal prosecution involves stress, uncertainty and the possibility of imprisonment. Extradition introduces additional burdens that may affect a mentally vulnerable person far more severely than an ordinary defendant.

For someone with serious depression, autism spectrum disorder accompanied by acute anxiety, psychosis or another significant psychiatric condition, extradition may involve:

* abrupt removal from family members and long-standing carers;
* disruption of established psychiatric treatment and medication;
* detention in an unfamiliar legal and healthcare system;
* communication difficulties with new clinicians and lawyers;
* prolonged uncertainty while awaiting trial in another country; and
* a foreseeable deterioration in [mental health]({{ 'mental-health/' | relative_url }}), including an increased risk of self-harm or suicide.

The legal concern is the cumulative effect of these factors. Courts are not simply asking whether prison healthcare exists in the requesting country, but whether the process of extradition itself would expose the individual to a real and unacceptable risk of catastrophic harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

Gary McKinnon's case illustrates this distinction. The Home Secretary did not conclude that he should avoid prosecution because he had Asperger's syndrome. Rather, after reviewing extensive psychiatric evidence and obtaining independent medical advice, she concluded that extradition would create such a high risk of suicide that surrender would be incompatible with his [human rights]({{ 'human-rights/' | relative_url }}). Domestic prosecution remained a separate possibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

## The difference between prosecution and extradition harm

One of the most common misunderstandings is to treat opposition to extradition as opposition to accountability. Legally, these are separate questions.

A defendant may still be capable of standing trial while nevertheless facing disproportionate harm from removal abroad. The additional burdens created by extradition include:

* **Geographical separation.** Family members who provide emotional support may be unable to visit regularly because of distance or cost.
* **Loss of clinical continuity.** Long-term treating psychiatrists and psychologists cannot simply transfer their therapeutic relationship to another [jurisdiction]({{ 'jurisdiction/' | relative_url }}).
* **Extended pre-trial detention.** Some extradition cases involve lengthy periods before trial, increasing uncertainty and psychological strain.
* **Different custodial practices.** Even where medical treatment is available, prison regimes, observation procedures and mental health services may differ substantially.

These factors matter because psychiatric deterioration is often driven not by a single event but by the interaction of isolation, uncertainty and disrupted support. A court therefore evaluates foreseeable consequences for the individual rather than assuming that all defendants will respond in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

## Factors courts should examine before surrender

Modern extradition decisions involving mental illness are highly individualised. Diagnosis alone is rarely decisive.

Courts generally place significant weight on questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* the quality, independence and consistency of psychiatric evidence;
* documented history of suicide attempts or self-harm;
* previous deterioration during periods of stress or detention;
* whether removal would interrupt effective treatment;
* the person's ability to cope with unfamiliar custodial conditions;
* availability of equivalent psychiatric care after extradition;
* whether protective measures would genuinely reduce the risk rather than merely contain it; and
* whether prosecution could realistically occur in the person's home jurisdiction instead.

</div>

This approach avoids two opposite errors. The first is assuming that any mental illness automatically prevents extradition. The second is assuming that the existence of prison medical services necessarily removes the risk. Psychiatric experts frequently distinguish between treatment being theoretically available and treatment being capable of preventing foreseeable deterioration in a particular individual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>

## Why foreign treatment assurances may not settle the issue

Requesting states often provide diplomatic or prosecutorial assurances that appropriate medical treatment will be available after extradition. These assurances are important, but they are not automatically decisive.

The central question is whether the assurances address the actual mechanism of harm.

For example, an assurance that psychiatric medication will be available does not necessarily answer concerns about:

* the psychological impact of forced removal itself;
* prolonged isolation from established support networks;
* deterioration triggered by uncertainty before trial;
* heightened [suicide risk]({{ 'suicide-risk/' | relative_url }}) during transport or early detention; or
* the practical ability of prison healthcare to manage an individual with unusually complex needs.

The debate therefore extends beyond the existence of medical facilities. Decision-makers must consider whether those facilities realistically prevent the specific risks identified by independent clinicians.

Earlier stages of McKinnon's litigation illustrate this point. At one stage, courts accepted American assurances regarding prison conditions and healthcare yet concluded that the legal threshold for blocking extradition had not been met. Later, following additional medical assessment and a different evidential picture, the Home Secretary reached the opposite conclusion because the predicted suicide risk was judged to be substantially higher. The change reflected evolving evidence rather than a general rejection of foreign medical assurances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2009-12-01/debates/09120144000002/GaryMckinnon%28Extradition%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hansard.parliament.uk">[Hansard]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hansard.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hansard Gary Mc Kinnon (Extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gary McKinnon (Extradition) - Hansard - UK Parliament...</span></span></span>

## Why McKinnon's case remains significant

The lasting importance of Gary McKinnon's case lies in the principle it illustrates rather than in any suggestion that autism or depression automatically bar extradition.

His case demonstrates that proportionality depends upon the interaction between an individual's psychiatric condition and the practical consequences of surrender. Serious criminal allegations remain capable of prosecution, but extradition may become unlawful where credible medical evidence shows that removal itself creates an exceptional and foreseeable risk of inhuman treatment through severe psychiatric collapse or suicide.

For later debates about vulnerable defendants, the case provides a framework rather than a blanket rule. Mental illness is neither an automatic shield from prosecution nor an irrelevant personal circumstance. It is a factor requiring careful, evidence-based assessment of whether the additional burdens imposed by extradition exceed what a democratic legal system can proportionately require.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/theresa-may-statement-on-gary-mckinnon-extradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theresa May statement on Gary Mc Kinnon extradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theresa May statement on Gary McKinnon extradition - GOV.UKOctober 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2012</span></span></span>
