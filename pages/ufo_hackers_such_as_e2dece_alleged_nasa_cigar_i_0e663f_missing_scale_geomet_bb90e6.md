## Introduction

One of the most important limitations of Gary McKinnon's account of the alleged cigar-shaped [NASA image]({{ 'nasa-image/' | relative_url }}) is also one of the simplest: he said the image provided no reliable way to determine the object's size. By his own description, he saw a partially rendered still image during a slow remote connection, without access to the original file, its metadata or the camera information. He explicitly recalled that there was "no reference to the size of the object".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://boingboing.net/2006/06/21/interview-with-ufo-h.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: boingboing.net">[Boing Boing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">boingboing.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boing Boing Interview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary Mc Kinnon: why&#x27;d he do it?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boing BoingInterview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary McKinnon: why&#x27;d he do it? - Boing BoingJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

It means the image, even if accurately remembered, cannot distinguish between a relatively small object close to the camera, a conventional spacecraft at an intermediate distance, or a much larger object much farther away. Without independent measurements, the apparent length of a silhouette in a photograph says almost nothing about its true physical dimensions.

## How Distance Changes Apparent Size

The central geometric problem is that photographs collapse a three-dimensional scene into two dimensions.

Two very different situations can produce nearly identical images:

* A small object located close to the camera.
* A much larger object located proportionally farther away.

Because both occupy the same angular size in the camera's field of view, they can appear identical in a single still photograph.

This ambiguity is familiar across astronomy, aviation and satellite imaging. Without independent distance measurements, apparent size alone cannot reveal actual dimensions. Modern computer-vision research likewise treats absolute scale as an inherently ambiguous property of a single image unless additional assumptions or reference objects are available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09529" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Single View Metrology in the Wild</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Single View Metrology in the WildJuly 18, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 18, 2020</span></span></span>

In McKinnon's case, there was no verified distance estimate and no documented reference object from which one could be derived.

Some retellings suggest that because Earth was visible beneath the alleged object, the object's size could be estimated. That conclusion does not follow.

The visible portion of Earth does not automatically establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how far the camera was from Earth,
* whether the object was near the camera or near Earth,
* whether the object lay above, below or far beyond the planet's apparent edge,
* whether the image had been cropped or digitally zoomed.

</div>

Perspective can make nearby objects appear dramatically larger than distant ones, while telephoto lenses can compress apparent distances and make widely separated objects appear close together. Without the original image geometry, none of these possibilities can be excluded.

Consequently, the presence of Earth in the frame does not convert the photograph into a measurable scene.

## What McKinnon's Own Account Supports

Importantly, McKinnon himself did not claim to know the object's dimensions. His description consistently stopped short of estimating its size, instead emphasising that there was no reference by which it could be judged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://boingboing.net/2006/06/21/interview-with-ufo-h.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: boingboing.net">[Boing Boing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">boingboing.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boing Boing Interview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary Mc Kinnon: why&#x27;d he do it?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boing BoingInterview with &quot;UFO Hacker&quot; Gary McKinnon: why&#x27;d he do it? - Boing BoingJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

That restraint is significant because later retellings sometimes describe the alleged object as an enormous spacecraft. Those descriptions go beyond what McKinnon reported. Based on his account alone, the image could not establish whether the object was metres long, tens of metres long or substantially larger.

## The Evidential Consequence

The inability to estimate size substantially limits what can be inferred from the alleged image.

Without scale, camera metadata or independent corroborating imagery, the silhouette cannot reliably distinguish between several possibilities, including nearby debris, a conventional spacecraft component, a satellite, an image artefact or a much larger distant object. Size therefore cannot be used as evidence either for or against an extraordinary interpretation.

For that reason, the missing scale is not merely an inconvenient omission—it is one of the principal reasons the alleged NASA image remains technically unverifiable. Even if McKinnon's recollection of what he saw was entirely sincere, the surviving description lacks the geometric information required to determine how large the object actually was.
