## Introduction

One of the most frequently cited damage allegations in the prosecution of Gary McKinnon concerns the claim that deleting critical files from just nine computers caused the US Army's Military District of Washington (MDW) network—serving more than 2,000 computers—to become unavailable for approximately 24 hours. This allegation became a central example used by US prosecutors to argue that McKinnon's actions went beyond [unauthorised access]({{ 'unauthorised-access/' | relative_url }}) and amounted to operational sabotage. At the same time, it is also one of the allegations that was never tested in a criminal trial, leaving the public record largely dependent on indictments, court summaries prepared for extradition proceedings, and official government statements rather than adversarial evidence.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mckinnon_damage_alle_511872_military_district_sh_37fbcf-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Network Shutdown illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mckinnon_damage_alle_511872_military_district_sh_37fbcf-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_hackers_such_as_e2dece_mckinnon_damage_alle_511872_military_district_sh_37fbcf-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The significance of this claim lies not only in the alleged disruption itself but in what it represented. If accurate, it showed that apparently small changes to a handful of systems could cascade across a large military network. If overstated, it illustrates the limits of relying solely on prosecution summaries when no trial establishes the underlying facts.

## What prosecutors said was deleted

The allegation appears consistently across the US indictment, the UK extradition litigation, and later government statements.

According to the prosecution, McKinnon gained administrative access to computers within the Military District of Washington network and deleted "critical operating system files" from nine computers. Prosecutors argued that these files were essential for normal operation and that their removal disabled the affected systems. More importantly, they alleged that the failures propagated through the wider network, shutting down the entire Military District of Washington network of more than 2,000 computers for approximately 24 hours and significantly disrupting government functions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

The House of Lords judgment, summarising the prosecution case for extradition, described the allegation in almost identical terms:

* critical operating-system files were deleted from nine computers;
* the deletion allegedly shut down the Military District of Washington network;
* the network served more than 2,000 computers;
* the outage allegedly lasted 24 hours; and
* government operations were said to have been significantly disrupted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

Although these facts were repeated in judicial decisions, the courts were recounting the American allegations rather than making factual findings after hearing evidence.

## How the shutdown allegedly spread

At first glance, the allegation appears disproportionate: nine affected computers causing disruption to more than 2,000 machines. However, prosecutors did not claim that all 2,000 computers were individually damaged.

Instead, their theory depended on the architecture of military computer networks.

The indictment alleged that the compromised machines occupied important positions within the Military District of Washington infrastructure rather than functioning as isolated desktop computers. Deleting operating-system files from network servers or other critical infrastructure could prevent authentication, file services or network management from functioning normally, making large numbers of connected systems effectively unusable even if those individual machines remained physically intact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because computer-crime statutes generally treat loss of system availability as damage, even where hardware survives and deleted software can eventually be restored.

Publicly available court documents, however, do not identify:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* precisely which nine computers were affected;
* the specific operating-system files allegedly deleted;
* the exact network topology involved; or
* the technical mechanism by which the outage propagated.

</div>

Those details have never been released in sufficient depth to permit independent technical reconstruction of the incident.

## Why the reported duration varies

Readers sometimes encounter different versions of the same allegation.

The House of Lords judgment, later Home Office summaries, and many news accounts describe a **24-hour shutdown** affecting more than 2,000 computers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

The original 2002 Department of Justice announcement, however, stated that McKinnon caused a Washington-area military network to shut down, resulting in the loss of internet access and email service to approximately 2,000 users **for three days**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

The publicly available record does not explain this discrepancy.

Possible explanations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* investigators refining the allegation during later proceedings;
* different measurements of outage versus restoration;
* distinctions between complete network shutdown and the restoration of all services; or
* simple differences in how prosecutors summarised the same event.

</div>

No publicly available judicial finding resolves which duration most accurately reflects what occurred.

## What the public record does not prove

The Military District of Washington allegation became one of the prosecution's strongest examples of operational damage, yet important evidential gaps remain.

First, no criminal trial examined the government's technical evidence through expert testimony or cross-examination. The extradition proceedings assumed, for procedural purposes, that the prosecution case was capable of supporting criminal charges if proved, rather than determining whether every allegation had in fact been proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200708/ldjudgmt/jd080730/mckinn-1.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publications.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publications.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament House of Lords</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentHouse of Lords - Mckinnon V Government of The United States of America and Another...</span></span></span>

Second, the detailed forensic evidence underlying the allegation has not entered the public domain. While prosecutors consistently referred to deleted operating-system files, they have not publicly released forensic reports identifying the files, explaining precisely how their deletion disabled the network, or documenting the recovery process.

Third, McKinnon admitted obtaining unauthorised access to US government computers but disputed deliberately causing serious damage. His public account portrayed himself as searching for information about UFOs and advanced technology rather than attempting to sabotage military systems. That denial does not itself disprove the prosecution's allegations, but it means the central factual dispute was never resolved by a verdict after contested evidence.

## Why this allegation became so important

Among the numerous [damage claims]({{ 'damage-claims/' | relative_url }}) in the McKinnon prosecution, the Military District of Washington outage stood out because it transformed the narrative from unauthorised access into alleged operational interference with military infrastructure.

Unlike allegations involving copied passwords or viewed files, the claimed shutdown suggested immediate consequences for government operations. It therefore featured prominently in indictments, extradition litigation, parliamentary discussion in the United Kingdom, and official government summaries over many years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/criminal/cybercrime/press-releases/2002/mckinnonIndict.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeLondon, England Hacker Indicted Under Computer Fraud and Abuse Act For Accessing Military Computers (November 12, 20...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the allegation remains an accusation rather than a judicially established historical fact. The public evidence demonstrates that US prosecutors consistently asserted that deleting critical operating-system files from nine computers disabled a network serving more than 2,000 machines for roughly a day. What the public record does not establish is the complete technical chain of causation or provide a trial-tested determination that those events occurred exactly as alleged.
