## Introduction

Gary McKinnon has said that, after viewing an alleged spreadsheet titled *Non-Terrestrial Officers*, he searched for the listed officers and could not find them in publicly available records. He took that apparent absence as one reason to suspect the names belonged to a hidden military organisation. The difficulty is that this observation cannot bear the weight often placed upon it. McKinnon never published the names, researchers cannot repeat his search, and there is no evidence that the public military directories available at the time were comprehensive enough for a failed search to demonstrate that the officers did not officially exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

The question is therefore not whether McKinnon searched, but whether his inability to locate the names is meaningful evidence. On the available record, the answer is that it is suggestive only if several assumptions are accepted in advance, and none of those assumptions can presently be verified.

## What McKinnon said he searched

In his best-known interview, McKinnon explained that the spreadsheet contained names and ranks of United States Air Force personnel whom he believed were "not registered anywhere else". He also said that the associated ship names could not be found through his own searches. When asked whether the document might instead have related to a military game or hypothetical planning exercise, he acknowledged that such an explanation was possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2006/06/ufo-hacker-tells-what-he-found/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo hacker tells what he found</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hacker&#x27; Tells What He Found &#124; WIREDJune 21, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 2006</span></span></span>

That qualification matters because McKinnon's claim combines two separate propositions:

* he remembered seeing names that appeared genuine;
* he could not later locate those names in public sources.

Only the second proposition can, in principle, be tested independently. However, no independent researcher knows which names were supposedly on the spreadsheet because they have never been released.

## Why military directories are incomplete

A common retelling of the story assumes that every serving military officer should appear in an easily searchable public database. That assumption does not match how United States military personnel records work.

Current and historical personnel information is spread across numerous repositories, service branches and administrative systems. Many records are not digitised, many require formal requests, and access frequently depends on privacy rules, archival status or the specific period of service. Even the National Archives emphasises that most military personnel records are not available online and often require individual requests rather than simple name searches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/veterans" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesMilitary and Veterans Research and Resources at The National Archives &#124; National ArchivesFebruary 16, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 16, 2022</span></span></span>

Similarly, the Department of Defense explains that official personnel files are maintained within dedicated military record systems rather than comprehensive public search portals. Public access varies according to whether the individual is serving, retired or deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/Contact/Help-Center/Article/Article/2742452/requesting-military-records-including-dd-form-214/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarRequesting Military Records Including DD Form-214 &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; ArticleAugust 23, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 2021</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/Contact/Help-Center/Article/Article/2742452/requesting-military-records-including-dd-form-214/)

This creates several alternative explanations for an unsuccessful search:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the officer's records were never publicly indexed;
* the records were not digitised at the time;
* the search relied on incomplete commercial or internet databases;
* the spelling, initials or service branch differed from what was remembered;
* the individual served under circumstances that limited publicly available information.

</div>

None of these possibilities requires the existence of a secret space programme.

## The missing names cannot be independently tested

The largest obstacle is methodological rather than historical.

McKinnon has never published:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the names he remembered;
* service numbers;
* dates of assignment;
* exact ranks;
* unit identifiers;
* screenshots of the spreadsheet.

</div>

Without those details, nobody can repeat the search or evaluate whether the names truly failed to appear in public records.

This is an important distinction between anecdotal evidence and reproducible evidence. If ten researchers cannot locate a named officer after searching the same archives, that finding can be evaluated. If the names themselves are unavailable, the claim remains dependent entirely upon memory.

## Why absence from public records is weak evidence

Researchers frequently caution against treating an inability to find a government record as evidence that something was intentionally concealed.

Military organisations generate records for administrative purposes, not for public discoverability. The absence of an online entry therefore tells readers very little by itself. Even agencies processing Freedom of Information Act requests require requesters to identify records with considerable specificity because personnel records are not organised as universal public directories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dfas.mil/foia/Information-Required-to-Conduct-a-Search/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dfas.mil">[Defense Finance and Accounting Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dfas.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Finance and Accounting Service Information Required to Conduct a Search</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Finance and Accounting ServiceInformation Required to Conduct a SearchFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

The logical problem is straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* If an officer appears in public records, that supports the conclusion that the individual existed.
* If an officer does **not** appear in the records searched, several ordinary explanations remain possible.
* Therefore, failure to find the name is not, by itself, evidence that the officer belonged to a classified organisation.

</div>

This is a classic example of the difference between "absence of evidence" and "evidence of absence."

## What evidence would make the claim testable?

The underlying claim could become much stronger if independent researchers could examine objective evidence rather than recollections.

Examples of evidence that would materially change the discussion include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the original spreadsheet or a forensic copy;
* authenticated screenshots showing the complete names;
* system logs confirming where the document was stored;
* corroboration from another person with independent access to the same file;
* successful identification of officers whose careers could then be verified or disproved through archival research.

</div>

None of this evidence has been produced publicly.

As a result, the claim that the listed officers "vanished" from public records remains unverified. What can be established is only that McKinnon reported unsuccessful searches after viewing an alleged spreadsheet.
